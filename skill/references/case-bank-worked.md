# Case Bank — Compact Worked Drills (Breadth Companion)

A wide, sector-diverse bank of case drills in **compact form** — the breadth companion to `practice-cases-quantified.md` (which carries a smaller set of full-length, deeply worked cases). Each drill gives: the **setup**, the **opening structure** that cracks it, the **quantified spine or key lever**, the **"aha"** that closes it, and the **trap** most people fall into.

These are re-engineered into native structure/quantification — reason *from* them, don't recite them. Numbers tagged **[ILLUSTRATIVE]** are demonstrative; swap in the real figures. Pairs with `case-pattern-library.md` (archetypes), `case-types.md` (framework selection), `guesstimates-and-frameworks-quantified.md` (sizing), and `structured-case-approach.md` (the Evolved "isolate before you explain" move, which several of these reward).

**How to use as an interviewer/coach:** pick a drill, give only the setup, and release each data point when the candidate asks for the right node. Grade the structure against the "opening structure," push toward the "spine," and coach the "trap."

## Part A — Profitability & Turnaround

### A1. Teak/timber producer — profits are *too high* (is it sustainable?)
**Setup.** A hardwood-plank producer earns profits well above competitors, ever since it started 3 years ago. The client worries the advantage isn't sustainable. Find the source and test durability.
**Structure.** This is a profitability case *run in reverse* — benchmark our profit structure vs. competitors to find the advantage, then stress its durability. Advantage sits in **price realised** or **cost per unit**; each can be structural or fluky.
**Spine.**
```
Profit/unit gap vs. peers = (our price − peer price) + (peer cost − our cost)
Decompose: are we selling more of the sturdier premium grade (mix)?  [price side]
           do we own better/cheaper forest plots or yield more planks/tree?  [cost side]
Then ask of each edge: is it OWNED (land, yield tech) or TEMPORARY (a spot-price spike, a lease about to reset)?
```
**Aha.** "Why are profits high?" is the same disaggregation as "why are profits low" — you're hunting a price or cost driver. The real question is **durability**: an owned structural edge (superior plots, higher yield) is defensible; a transient one (favourable input prices, a soon-expiring lease) is not.
**Trap.** Treating "high profits" as good news to celebrate rather than a driver to decompose and stress-test.

### A2. Illicit-goods smuggling network — profits falling in one region
**Setup.** A cross-border contraband operation (dark-humour framing of a classic value-chain case) reports falling profits over 2 years, concentrated in one export region while rivals are steady.
**Structure.** Rival stability + regional concentration ⇒ a **company-specific, geography-specific** problem. Units produced are constant but units *sold* are down ⇒ **isolate along the value chain**: Production → cross-border transport → in-market distributor push → end demand.
**Spine.** Production unchanged; find which link leaks. If product reaches the region but not the street, the break is **distribution-push** (a corridor shut down, a distributor defected) not demand.
**Aha.** The Evolved move in disguise: constant production + falling sales = a push/logistics break somewhere downstream. Isolate the link before theorising about end-customer preference.
**Trap.** Jumping to "customers want less" when the market and rivals say demand is fine.

### A3. Fruit-juice manufacturer — losses since inception, rivals profitable
**Setup.** A 4-year-old packaged-juice maker (sells 1L and 250ml through small/medium/large retailers) has *never* been profitable, while every competitor is. Turn it around.
**Structure.** "Loss-making since day one while peers profit" ⇒ **company-specific, structural**. Benchmark our P&L vs. peers on both sides: is our **price** too low or **cost/unit** too high? Segment by **pack size** (250ml vs 1L often have very different margins and channel economics).
**Spine.**
```
Contribution/unit by SKU = price − variable cost
Small packs: high price/litre but high packaging & distribution cost/litre
If we over-index on a low-contribution SKU or a low-margin channel → structural loss
Fixed-cost absorption: at 10% share, is volume even above break-even scale?
```
**Aha.** A never-profitable business with profitable peers is almost always a **structural cost or scale problem** (sub-scale plant, wrong SKU/channel mix), not a demand problem.
**Trap.** Chasing revenue growth when the unit economics are upside-down — growth just scales the loss.

### A4. Spirits (vodka) maker — long slow profit decline, sharp last year
**Setup.** Single-product vodka maker; profits declining ~10 years, sharply in the last year; industry also down but less. Diagnose and fix.
**Structure.** Two-clock problem: a **slow structural decline** (secular) plus a **recent shock**. Split profit into volume-driven and cost-driven; the setup hints at **falling units** *and* a **jump in distribution cost**.
**Spine.** Address separately: (1) why volume fell (category shift away from vodka? share loss?), (2) why distribution cost spiked last year (a distributor renegotiated? route economics changed?). Size which one moved profit more before prescribing.
**Aha.** When a metric has both a long trend and a recent break, treat them as **two problems with two causes** — don't let one narrative explain both.
**Trap.** Blaming "the industry" — the client is falling *faster* than the industry, so part of it is company-specific.

### A5. Consumer-electronics maker — revenue −20% in one product
**Setup.** Kitchen-appliance maker (microwaves, fridges, toasters) sees revenue −20% over a year while the category grows +5%. The drop is concentrated in one line (toasters, −22%).
**Structure.** Category up, us down ⇒ **share loss, company-specific**. Isolate the losing SKU, then run the value chain: **Production → distribution/retail push → customer pull**.
**Spine.** A revealed fact: the plant was shut ~2 months for triennial maintenance → a **production/supply constraint**, not a demand collapse. Quantify: 2/12 downtime ≈ 17% capacity lost, closely tracking the 22% unit drop.
**Aha.** A double-digit drop that suspiciously matches a **supply outage** is a production story — check the boring operational cause before building a demand thesis.
**Trap.** Elaborate customer/competitor theories when a maintenance shutdown explains almost the whole gap.

### A6. Highway petrol pump — falling profit, area-wide
**Setup.** Owner of 50 stations; one on a Delhi–Chandigarh highway is losing money; broadly all area pumps are down too; been happening a few years.
**Structure.** Area-wide + multi-year ⇒ **industry/structural**, not one bad manager. Profit → transactions × margin/transaction. Transactions = (vehicles in/through area) × (% that fill) × (our share).
**Spine.** An area-wide multi-year fall in throughput points to **fewer vehicles passing** (a new bypass/expressway rerouted traffic) or **share loss to a new competitor**. Test the traffic-rerouting hypothesis first — it's the structural killer.
**Aha.** "All pumps in the area are down" reframes the case from *operations* to *catchment* — the demand pool physically shrank (road rerouting), which no in-station fix repairs.
**Trap.** Optimising forecourt operations when the cars simply stopped driving past.

### A7. PE-owned retail chain — revenue 2×, but margin 40%→35%
**Setup.** After a PE acquisition, a retail chain opened 10 new stores (30→40) in a year; revenue doubled but profitability fell 5 pts. Why, and what to do?
**Structure.** Revenue up, margin down = classic **mix / deleverage / ramp** question. Segment by **store cohort** (mature vs. new), product category, and geography.
**Spine.**
```
New stores ramp below maturity: lower sales density, full fixed cost (rent, staff, D&A) from day 1
Blended margin = w_mature·m_mature + w_new·m_new  ; m_new << m_mature during ramp
So a −5pt blended margin can be entirely a MIX-of-immature-stores effect, not deterioration of the core
```
**Aha.** Rapid store expansion **mechanically dilutes margin** while new units ramp — the core may be perfectly healthy. Separate "ramp drag" (temporary, expected) from "core erosion" (real problem) before acting.
**Trap.** Cutting costs at healthy mature stores to fix a number that's really just immature-store drag.

### A8. One-year civic mega-event (sponsored pavilions) — losing money, 10 months to fix
**Setup.** A government-run exhibition (17 themed, sponsor-funded zones + ticketed entry) is losing money and drawing bad press; goal is break-even, not profit; asset dies in 10 months.
**Structure.** Two objectives — **break-even** and **reputation** — and a hard clock. Revenue = sponsor fees + ticket sales; the short life and sunk build cost mean **only near-term levers matter**.
**Spine.** Break-even = fixed run-cost ÷ contribution. Pull the fast levers: re-fill empty zones (sponsor yield), raise footfall/ticket yield (events, marketing), cut variable run-cost. Reputation and footfall reinforce each other.
**Aha.** With a fixed short remaining life and sunk capex, ignore long-term strategy — **maximise contribution over the 10 months** against a break-even bar, and treat publicity as a demand lever, not a side issue.
**Trap.** Proposing structural/brand investments that can't pay back inside 10 months.

### A9. Single-venue theatre — grow profit over 5 years, same building
**Setup.** A one-venue playhouse wants maximum profit growth over 5 years, but won't build new venues or change the venue's purpose.
**Structure.** Growth boxed to one asset ⇒ **sweat the asset**: yield (ticket price, tiering, dynamic pricing), utilisation (more shows/slots, off-peak, weekday), and **ancillary revenue** (F&B, memberships, sponsorship, venue hire between shows).
**Spine.** Profit = (shows × seats × occupancy × price) − fixed venue cost + ancillary contribution. With capacity fixed, the levers are **occupancy × yield × ancillary**, since seats and venue cost are largely fixed.
**Aha.** A capacity-constrained venue grows profit through **yield management and ancillary streams**, not volume — every incremental seat/ancillary rupee is almost pure contribution against a fixed cost base.
**Trap.** Defaulting to "sell more tickets" when the room size caps volume; missing the high-margin ancillary pools.

### A10. Luxury hotel chain — tourists +6%, revenue +4% (the gap)
**Setup.** A 20-property luxury resort chain: national tourist arrivals grew 6% but chain revenue only 4%. Find the 2-pt gap.
**Structure.** RevPAR logic: revenue = rooms × occupancy × ADR. A demand tailwind not fully captured ⇒ leak in **occupancy** (did we lose share of arrivals?) or **ADR/mix** (did rates soften, or did the growth land in a *budget* segment we don't serve?).
**Spine.** Segment tourist growth by tier — if the +6% is disproportionately **budget/mid-scale** travellers, a luxury-only chain structurally under-captures it. Alternatively, share loss to new luxury supply caps our occupancy.
**Aha.** Market growth ≠ your growth when the growth is in a **segment you don't serve**. The gap is often a **mix mismatch**, not an operational failure.
**Trap.** Assuming any positive market growth should flow through proportionally; not segmenting *where* the growth actually occurred.

## Part B — Market Entry & New Product

*Common spine for entry cases:* `Profit = Market size × Share × Contribution/unit − Investment`, plus a **value-chain feasibility** check (can we actually build/distribute/service it?). Decide on a metric (profit, break-even by year N) *first*.

### B1. Telecom operator → home-security alarms (diversification)
**Setup.** A telecom wants to enter electronic home-security (alarm + sensors), targeting ≥5% share in year 1; estimate profit and how to get the share.
**Structure.** Size market (households → security-relevant segment → penetration), apply a defensible 5% share, contribution/unit, less investment. Share plan leans on the telecom's **existing distribution and customer base** (the "right to win").
**Aha.** The diversification angle is the **synergy with the core** — an entrant with an installed customer base and distribution has a credible path to share that a pure startup doesn't. Anchor the 5% on that asset.
**Trap.** Sizing the market but never justifying *how* 5% share is won; ignoring installation/after-sales as part of the value chain.

### B2. Semi-automatic washing machine, 1990 market entry into a new state
**Setup.** An electronics maker wants to introduce a patented semi-automatic washing machine into a new regional market (period setting: early-1990s, few substitutes); benchmark expected profit vs. its home market.
**Structure.** Profit = size × share × margin, **plus** a value-chain build (can we distribute/service a new state?). Patent + few substitutes ⇒ **temporary monopoly**, so share can be high but **adoption is gated by awareness and affordability** in an early market.
**Aha.** In an under-penetrated market with a patented product, the binding constraint isn't competition — it's **category creation** (awareness, financing, distribution reach), so model a slow adoption ramp, not instant share.
**Trap.** Assuming a patent = instant high volume; ignoring that the value chain (dealers, service) must be built from scratch.

### B3. Pharma anti-smoking pill — enter, break-even in 2 years
**Setup.** A drugmaker with a prescription anti-smoking pill (daily for 4 months; ~50% quit permanently) wants to enter, maximise profit, and at least break even in 2 years on a ~₹100M plant investment.
**Structure.** Market size = smokers → who'd seek to quit → who'd get a prescription → realistic share. Profit = units × (price − VC) − fixed/investment; test break-even ≤ 2 yrs. **Prescription** status routes go-to-market through **doctors**, not shelves.
**Spine.**
```
Units = market size × share ; contribution = price − variable cost
Break-even units = investment ÷ contribution/unit  → is that share reachable in 2 yrs?
```
**Aha.** For a prescription product, **physician adoption is the funnel bottleneck** — the entry plan is a detailing/prescriber strategy, and the go/no-go is whether break-even share is achievable inside the 2-year window.
**Trap.** Marketing to consumers for a prescription drug; treating market size as addressable demand without the prescription funnel.

### B4. Solar lantern into an emerging/BoP market
**Setup.** An electronics company with a solar lantern wants to know whether to enter a large developing market; it has a target price in mind and wants profit potential + go-to-market.
**Structure.** Size the **off-grid / unreliable-power** population (the real addressable base), realistic share, contribution at the target price, less investment. Value chain: reaching rural/last-mile customers is the hard part.
**Aha.** For a BoP product, the addressable market is **not the whole population** — it's the electricity-poor segment, and **affordability + last-mile distribution** (financing, rural channels) decide success more than the product.
**Trap.** Sizing to total population instead of the off-grid segment; underestimating distribution cost to reach dispersed low-income buyers.

### B5. Reusable "spaceplane" / novel high-capex transport — enter & price
**Setup.** A firm has developed a novel reusable air/space transport that takes off and lands like a plane; assess the market and how to price a radically new category.
**Structure.** No comparables ⇒ price on **value to customer / willingness-to-pay**, not cost-plus. Size by use case (cargo priority, ultra-premium travel), each with its own WTP ceiling.
**Aha.** For a first-of-kind, **cost sets the floor, customer value sets the ceiling** — price is chosen in that band by segment WTP, and the market is the sum of use cases whose value clears the cost floor.
**Trap.** Cost-plus pricing on a category that has no cost precedent and huge value dispersion; sizing "everyone who flies."

### B6. Snow-shovel importer/distributor — stable market, break-even focus
**Setup.** An importer/distributor of a seasonal, weather-dependent commodity (snow shovels) in a stable market wants to understand profit and break-even.
**Structure.** Highly **seasonal + weather-variable demand** ⇒ model in ranges (mild vs. harsh winter), fixed import/warehousing cost vs. per-unit contribution, break-even units, and **inventory risk** (unsold stock at season end).
**Aha.** For a seasonal commodity, the real analysis is **break-even under demand variance and inventory risk**, not a point forecast — size a bad-winter downside and the carrying cost of overstock.
**Trap.** Using average-winter demand as if it were certain; ignoring the newsvendor-style overstock/understock cost.

## Part C — Pricing & Commercial Structure

### C1. Business-district hotel — raise room rate $160 → $180?
**Setup.** Uniform-rate business hotel considers a ~12.5% rate hike to lift profit. Recommend.
**Structure.** A pricing-elasticity + break-even question. Rooms are **near-fixed cost**, so a rate change flows mostly to profit *unless* occupancy drops. Find the **break-even occupancy loss**.
**Spine.**
```
New rate = 180 (+12.5%). Break-even occupancy ratio = old price / new price = 160/180 = 0.889
→ We can lose up to ~11% of occupancy and still match current room revenue.
Since incremental cost per occupied room is low, the true break-even occupancy drop is even larger.
Decision hinges on elasticity: will a 12.5% hike cost >~11% of occupancy in a business market?
```
**Aha.** With a mostly fixed cost base, the question reduces to **"how much occupancy can I lose and stay whole?"** — business travellers (low price sensitivity, expensed) usually make the hike accretive.
**Trap.** Debating the price in the abstract instead of computing the break-even occupancy loss and judging elasticity against it.

### C2. Pharma baldness cure — size the market, then value-price
**Setup.** A prescription daily pill regrows hair; estimate the market and set price; note a side-effect (dysfunction in ~2% and pregnancy risk).
**Structure.** Size the affected, treatment-seeking, prescription-reachable population; **defer income segmentation until price is set** (price drives affordability). Price on **value/WTP** with cost as floor and competitor/substitute prices as reference; discount the addressable base for side-effect avoiders.
**Aha.** Price and market size are **interdependent** for a value-priced drug — set price on WTP first (it defines who can afford it), then re-cut the size at that price; fold side-effect aversion into the funnel.
**Trap.** Sizing at a fixed price then pricing independently; ignoring that side effects shrink the willing population.

### C3. Fashion magazine — switch to targeted ads?
**Setup.** Monthly magazine, 1M copies, 100 ads/issue, ads priced ~$50 per 1,000 copies. Proposal: split into premium-ad and economy-ad editions targeted by reader class. Does targeting raise ad revenue?
**Structure.** Compare **current** blanket-ad revenue vs. **targeted** revenue, where targeting lets you **charge premium advertisers more for a higher-value audience** but shrinks each ad's reach.
**Spine.**
```
Current  = ads × copies × rate = 100 × (1M/1,000) × $50 = $5.0M/issue      [ILLUSTRATIVE]
Targeted: split 1M into premium (say 300k) and economy (700k) editions.
  Premium advertisers pay a higher CPM for a qualified audience; economy CPM flat/down.
  New = premium_ads×300k/1,000×rate_hi + economy_ads×700k/1,000×rate_lo
Targeting wins only if the premium uplift on the smaller qualified base beats the reach lost.
```
**Aha.** Targeting trades **reach for relevance** — it pays only if premium advertisers' CPM uplift on the qualified sub-audience outweighs the smaller circulation each ad now reaches.
**Trap.** Assuming "targeting is always better" without doing the reach-vs-CPM arithmetic.

### C4. Cigarette maker — outsource the distribution/trucking fleet?
**Setup.** A manufacturer considers outsourcing its in-house distribution trucking to a 3PL. Should it?
**Structure.** A **make-vs-buy / TCO** decision. Compare fully-loaded in-house cost (trucks, drivers, maintenance, depreciation, utilisation) vs. 3PL price, plus **strategic factors** (control, flexibility, service level, asset-light balance sheet).
**Aha.** Outsource when the 3PL's **scale/utilisation advantage** beats in-house fully-loaded cost *and* distribution isn't a source of competitive advantage worth owning; keep in-house if control/service is a differentiator.
**Trap.** Comparing 3PL price to in-house *cash* cost only, omitting depreciation, utilisation, and the strategic value of control.

## Part D — Deals, Defense & Contract Economics

### D1. Book retailer — switch from returnable to non-returnable supply (newsvendor)
**Setup.** A retailer of a *perishable* periodical currently returns unsold copies free (buy $10, sell $12, publisher cost $5). A new deal offers a **lower purchase price but no returns** — how many copies should the retailer commit to?
**Structure.** Classic **newsvendor**: with no returns, over-ordering wastes the full cost of unsold copies; under-ordering forgoes margin. Optimal order covers demand up to the **critical ratio**.
**Spine.**
```
Under returnable deal: no downside to unsold → order to cover peak demand.
Under non-returnable deal at lower cost c':
  Overage cost Co = c' (wasted per unsold)      Underage cost Cu = price − c' (lost margin)
  Critical fractile = Cu / (Cu + Co) → order the demand quantile at that fractile
Switch is worth it only if the lower cost c' expands margin enough to beat expected waste.
```
**Aha.** Removing returns transfers **demand risk to the retailer** — the right order quantity is a newsvendor fractile, and the deal is attractive only if the price cut compensates for expected unsold waste.
**Trap.** Comparing per-unit margins only, ignoring the cost of unsold inventory the retailer now eats.

### D2. #1 discount retailer — a foreign giant is acquiring the #2 (defend)
**Setup.** The market-leading discount retailer (500 stores) learns a much larger foreign chain (4,000 stores) is acquiring the domestic #2 (300 stores). How is the leader threatened, and what to do?
**Structure.** Threat = how the deal changes **share and profitability**. The acquirer imports **scale-driven cost advantage** (buying power, logistics) → potential **price war** the incumbent's margins may not survive. Assess on cost position, price elasticity, customer loyalty, and store overlap.
**Aha.** The threat isn't the extra 300 stores — it's the **cost curve** the global acquirer injects, enabling sustained lower prices. Defense = protect cost position (scale, private label), lock loyalty, and pre-empt in key catchments.
**Trap.** Counting stores instead of analysing the **cost/scale** shift that lets the combined rival underprice you durably.

### D3. Fuel wholesaler — lift our profit *without* hurting the downstream partner
**Setup.** A wholesale fuel supplier's business through an independent chain of ~100 stations has stagnated for years. Propose ways for the supplier to grow profit **without reducing the station operator's profit** (or they'll refuse).
**Structure.** A **win-win / vertical value** problem. The supplier can't just raise price (loses the contract). Grow the **joint pie**: raise throughput (footfall, non-fuel convenience sales, loyalty), cut shared costs (logistics, replenishment), or add higher-margin products — then split the upside so the operator is no worse off.
**Aha.** When you can't take margin *from* a partner, you must **expand the shared value** and let them keep their profit — the analysis is joint-P&L, not a zero-sum price move.
**Trap.** Proposing a price increase or margin grab that the partner would simply reject.

## Part E — Org, Operations & Abstract / Creative

### E1. Pharma sales force — cut sales-force cost 50% without wrecking coverage
**Setup.** Cut sales-force cost in half. Structure (salary units, "x"): CEO 20x×1; Regional Heads 10x×4; Area Heads 4x×24; Sales Reps 1x×240 — total ≈ **396x**.
**Structure.** Start at the **bottom of the pyramid** (reps do the value-adding work; every layer above exists to manage the layer below), because rep count sets how many managers you actually need. Levers: reduce headcount per layer, re-span, or trim pay.
**Spine.**
```
Total ≈ 396x.  Reps = 240x (61% of cost) → biggest lever, so start there.
Cut reps to the level justified by territory/workload → managers shrink proportionally via span.
e.g. halve reps to 120 → with span 10, Area Heads 12 (48x), with span 6 Regional Heads 2 (20x), CEO 20x
New ≈ 120 + 48 + 20 + 20 = 208x  → ~47% cut, mostly from resizing the base and cascading up.
```
**Aha.** In a hierarchy, **size the base to the work first**, then let spans cascade the cuts upward — attacking managers first breaks coverage and still leaves the big rep cost untouched.
**Trap.** Starting cuts at the top (small $) or slashing reps arbitrarily without tying headcount to territory workload.

### E2. Fixed-fleet route optimisation — reach a destination in least time
**Setup.** Using an existing car fleet, find the fastest route between two fixed points on a specific future date.
**Structure.** Time = distance ÷ speed → minimise time by acting on **distance** (route choice) and **speed** (congestion, road type, time-of-day, signals). Enumerate candidate routes; estimate each leg's realistic speed given predictable congestion on that date.
**Aha.** "Fastest" ≠ "shortest" — the win is **modelling time-varying speed** (traffic by hour, road class) across candidate routes, not just picking the least-kilometre path.
**Trap.** Optimising distance alone and ignoring congestion/time-of-day, which dominate real travel time.

### E3. Film greenlight — will this movie succeed?
**Setup.** A film studio has a script and must decide if the film will "succeed." Two success types: **commercial** (make profit) and **critical** (win acclaim, at least break even). This one is pitched as an artistic/critical project.
**Structure.** Define success *first*, then structure to it. Critical: assess **creative elements** (script, direction, cast, music, cinematography). Financial: revenue (box office by screens × occupancy × ticket + ancillary/rights) vs. production + marketing cost → break-even.
**Aha.** The objective **changes the whole tree** — for a critical film, judge creative quality against a **break-even** bar, not profit maximisation; nail down the success definition before analysing.
**Trap.** Running a generic profit model when the client's objective is critical acclaim with a break-even floor.

### E4. Monetise a unique found asset (options-structuring, abstract)
**Setup.** A client unexpectedly acquires a unique, valuable, slightly dangerous asset (playful framing: a live dinosaur) and wants maximum profit. What should they do with it?
**Structure.** **Enumerate options MECE**, then score each on value and feasibility: **sell** it (to whom — research, collectors?), **operate** it (exhibit/theme-park experience — fits an owner who already runs attractions), or **license/partner**. Weigh one-time cash vs. recurring income, risk, and capability fit.
**Aha.** Open "what should they do" cases are **option-generation-and-evaluation** problems — the score is a clean MECE option set (sell / operate / license) filtered by the client's existing capabilities (here: they already run attractions → operate).
**Trap.** Latching onto one idea instead of laying out the option space and choosing on value × feasibility × capability fit.

### E5. Prime land parcel — highest-and-best use with multiple objectives
**Setup.** A client holds a large, prime urban parcel (former industrial mill land) and wants, in priority order: (1) profit, (2) brand recognition, (3) space for philanthropy — plus a team of ex-operations managers to redeploy.
**Structure.** A **highest-and-best-use** option case with **weighted objectives**. Generate uses (mixed-use commercial, retail/mall, offices, residential, a landmark development), score each on the three ranked goals, and check capability fit for the retained managers.
**Aha.** With ranked multi-objectives, the answer is a **portfolio/zoning mix** that leads on profit while carving brand and philanthropic components — optimise the primary objective subject to satisfying the secondary ones, not any single goal alone.
**Trap.** Optimising one objective (usually profit) and ignoring the client's explicit secondary goals and the ranking between them.
