# India Sector Primers — Market Structure at a Glance

Fast, partner-level orientation for twenty-eight Indian sectors: the market shape, who leads, the value chain, the KPIs to speak in, the regulator, and the recurring case tension. Use immediately after problem framing on any India engagement or case, alongside `references/india-corporate-houses.md` (who the incumbents are) and `references/industry-heuristics.md` / `references/industry-intelligence.md` (global sector economics). Companion to `references/industry-heuristics.md`.

**All figures are approximate, directional benchmarks** for structuring, not sourced facts — market sizes and growth rates move, so refresh before quoting to a client and label anything you extrapolate `[ILLUSTRATIVE]`. **Figures are dated** (mostly ~FY25–FY26 or CY2025, as stated next to each number; FY = April–March) and go stale within a year, so re-check the latest release before any client use. Every size is only as good as its metric: read the **Size & growth** bullet (what is measured, as of when, over which window) before quoting it, and run the checks in [Sizing traps across sectors](#sizing-traps-across-sectors).

**How to read the newer bullets.** *Size & growth* defines the metric, value, as-of date and growth window. *Five forces* rates threat of **E**ntry / **S**upplier power / **B**uyer power / threat of **Sub**stitutes as L, M or H. Rivalry is high in almost every Indian sector, so it is left out: the other four forces are where sectors differ. *Cost vs revenue drivers* separates the lines that move margin from the lines that move the top line. *Key terms* lists the vocabulary to use in the room.

## Automobile
- **Snapshot.** A record ~28.3M vehicles sold domestically in FY26 (wholesale units). India is the world's 3rd-largest vehicle market by four-wheeler sales (it overtook Japan in CY2022), 4th by production, and the largest two-wheeler market. Four segments: two-wheelers and passenger vehicles dominate domestic demand, plus commercial vehicles and tractors. 2W outsell PVs ~4.7:1 (FY26). An older "~$220B, 5th-largest" line is stale; do not quote it.
- **Size & growth.** Metric: domestic wholesales (factory dispatches to dealers, units, all segments, industry-body data). 28.27M in FY26, +10.4% YoY; PVs 4.64M (+7.9%), 2W 21.71M (+10.7%, ~77% of volume). Retail registrations differ from wholesales by channel-inventory build or drawdown. Global rank uses calendar-year 4W (PV + CV) sales: India ~5.5M vs Japan ~4.6M in CY2025. For EV share, see [Electric vehicles (four-wheelers)](#electric-vehicles-four-wheelers).
- **Five forces.** Entry L-M (scale, dealer networks and localisation gate it; EV start-ups and global brands enter through duty concessions). Suppliers M (semiconductors, battery cells and some imported components; Tier-1 consolidation). Buyers M-H (price-sensitive and finance-dependent; fleets negotiate). Substitutes M (public transport, metro, ride-hailing).
- **Cost vs revenue drivers.** Cost: materials (steel, aluminium, electronics), compliance (BS-VI emission and safety norms), logistics, EV-platform R&D run alongside ICE lines, and the cost of financing dealer inventory. Revenue: income growth and rural demand, **vehicle-finance availability and interest rates** (demand is credit-led, so rate cycles move volume as much as income does), infrastructure-led CV demand, exports, and after-sales.
- **Key terms.** Wholesales vs retail (registrations), channel inventory days, vehicle density (vehicles per 1,000 people), replacement demand, localisation ratio, first-pass yield, warranty cost per vehicle, service revenue per customer, BS-VI, PLI.
- **Leaders.** Maruti Suzuki (car leader), Hyundai, Tata Motors, Mahindra (SUV/utility); 2W — Hero, Honda, TVS, Bajaj.
- **Value chain.** R&D → raw material → inbound logistics → manufacturing → outbound → dealer distribution → marketing → after-sales (service + parts, a high-margin annuity).
- **KPIs.** Volume growth, segment mix, capacity utilization, dealer inventory days, EBITDA margin, EV penetration.
- **Formulas.** On-time shipping rate = orders shipped by the requested date ÷ total orders. Incomplete vehicles at end of line = units held for missing parts (a supply-chain health signal).
- **Case tension.** The ICE→EV transition (capex + new competitors), rural demand cyclicality, and the after-sales profit pool. Watch localization/PLI incentives.

## Aviation
- **Snapshot.** 3rd-largest domestic market; low-cost-carrier led and now highly consolidated (IndiGo dominant; Air India–Vistara under Tata is #2). Segments: airlines, airports, MRO & ground handling.
- **Value chain.** Flight ops · ground ops · MRO (operational); revenue management · distribution · customer experience (commercial). Airport ownership splits AAI-managed vs. private-concession (Adani, GMR).
- **KPIs.** RASK/CASK, load factor, ASK growth, fuel cost % of opex (~40%), fleet utilization, on-time performance.
- **Formulas.** Seat load factor = RPK ÷ ASK (≈ passengers ÷ seats). Regularity = flights operated ÷ flights scheduled. On-time departure % = on-time departures ÷ flights operated.
- **Size & growth.** Two measures that must not be mixed. (a) Domestic airline passengers (aviation-regulator data, one count per flight segment): 167.7M in FY26, +1.4% YoY; 166.9M in CY2025, +3.5%. (b) Airport footfall (arrivals + departures, domestic + international): ~412M in FY25, which counts every domestic passenger at both ends, so its domestic portion is 2 × (a) (~330M); adding international traffic takes the total to ~2.5 × (a). Growth slowed from double digits in FY24–FY25 to ~1–4% in FY26 (the market leader's scheduling disruption, dearer ATF, airspace closures). Rank: 3rd-largest domestic market by seat capacity, after the US and China. Share (Jan–Oct 2025): IndiGo ~64%, Air India group ~27%; LCCs ~78% of domestic capacity.
- **Five forces.** Entry L-M (capital, slots at constrained metro airports, and a long list of failed carriers; lessors make aircraft easy to get). Suppliers **H** (two airframe makers, a few engine makers, lessors, ATF priced off global crude plus taxes, monopoly airport operators). Buyers H (fare aggregators make prices transparent; switching cost is low). Substitutes M (rail, including premium trains on routes under ~500 km, road, and video calls for business trips). Airlines are squeezed from both sides, which is why margins are chronically thin.
- **Cost vs revenue drivers.** Cost: ATF (~30–40% of opex), aircraft leases and maintenance (USD-linked), airport and navigation charges, crew. A large share of cost is in dollars while revenue is in rupees, so the exchange rate is a P&L line. Revenue: traffic growth, load factor × yield, ancillaries (seats, bags, meals), cargo, loyalty, and international expansion.
- **Key terms.** RPK, ASK, yield (revenue per RPK), RASK/CASK, ancillary revenue, hub-and-spoke vs point-to-point, slot, aircraft utilisation (block hours per day), wet vs dry lease, UDAN (regional-connectivity scheme), MRO.
- **Case tension.** Thin margins squeezed between fuel + lease costs and price-sensitive yields; route/network economics; the airport-privatization wave. A mandate for ~1% sustainable aviation fuel is on the horizon.

## Cement
- **Snapshot.** ~$143B; India is the world's 2nd-largest producer (~7%+ of global capacity). Top-4 players hold ~75%. Demand splits roughly urban/rural/infrastructure.
- **Process flow.** Limestone (extracted) → raw meal → clinker (kiln) → cement (+ gypsum) → bagged → retailers. Energy and freight are the cost swing factors.
- **Leaders.** UltraTech (Aditya Birla) #1, then Adani (ACC + Ambuja, acquired 2022 — the largest Indian infra M&A), Shree, Dalmia.
- **KPIs.** Capacity utilization, realization (₹/tonne), freight radius, power & fuel cost/tonne, blended EBITDA/tonne.
- **Case tension.** A commoditized, freight-bound, consolidating market — the game is utilization, cost position, and regional pricing discipline.

## E-commerce
- **Snapshot.** ~$65–66B of B2C e-retail GMV in CY2025, growing ~20% a year. Models span marketplace, inventory-led, and quick-commerce. Categories led by electronics, fashion, and grocery. Other trackers put online retail at ~$75–80B because they use a different year basis and scope, so name the tracker and scope before quoting.
- **Leaders.** Flipkart (Walmart) and Amazon at the top; Meesho on the value tier (zero-commission, social/reseller distribution); Blinkit/Instamart/Zepto in quick-commerce.
- **Value chain.** Sourcing/selection → catalog → demand generation → fulfilment/logistics (the cost battleground) → last mile → returns.
- **KPIs.** GMV, take rate, AOV, contribution margin per order, CAC/LTV, delivery cost per order, repeat rate.
- **Formulas.** Cart abandonment = 1 − completed orders ÷ carts created. Inventory turnover = COGS ÷ average inventory. Operating-expense ratio = opex ÷ revenue.
- **Size & growth.** Metric: B2C e-retail GMV, meaning physical goods sold online including quick commerce, and excluding travel, food delivery, digital services and B2B. ~$65–66B in CY2025, +19–21% YoY; quick commerce ~$10–11B of it (~16%). Outlook: ~$170–180B by 2030, which needs ~21–23% CAGR over five years. "$100B+ e-commerce" figures usually add travel, food delivery, digital services or B2B, so they are not comparable. Base: ~290–300M online shoppers (2025), the 2nd-largest after China, about 30% of internet users; tier-2+ towns give ~half of incremental orders; e-retail is only ~1.6% of GDP.
- **Five forces.** Entry M (a national marketplace needs logistics and capital, but ONDC, social commerce and D2C lower the bar at the edges). Suppliers M (sellers multi-home; large brands have leverage; FDI rules stop foreign-owned platforms from owning inventory). Buyers H (price comparison, cash on delivery, free returns, zero switching cost). Substitutes H (offline retail and kiranas; quick commerce for top-up baskets).
- **Cost vs revenue drivers.** Cost: fulfilment and last-mile logistics, returns and RTO (cash on delivery drives both and delays cash), CAC and discounts, technology, warehousing. Revenue: take rate (commission, fulfilment and advertising fees, with ads growing fastest), AOV, purchase frequency, fintech attach (pay-later, co-branded cards), private label. The prepaid share is a unit-economics lever.
- **Key terms.** GMV vs net merchandise value vs revenue, take rate, marketplace vs inventory-led model (FDI), COD share, RTO, reverse logistics, fulfilment centre, last mile, hyperlocal, drop shipping, omnichannel, ONDC.
- **Case tension.** Growth vs. contribution margin. India e-commerce cases live or die on **unit economics per order**, not GMV. FDI rules constrain inventory-led models for foreign players.

## Financial services (ex-banking)
- **Snapshot.** Deep and digitizing; the stock market is projected to approach ~$5T (top-5 globally). Segments: capital markets (broking, IB, AMC), insurance (life/non-life), and NBFCs.
- **Benchmarks.** MF AUM ~$478B with only ~5–6% penetration (long runway); insurance AUM heading toward ~$1T. FDI in insurance companies is now allowed up to 100% (raised from 74% by a December 2025 amendment); see [Insurance](#insurance).
- **Leaders.** Bajaj Finance (NBFC), HDFC/ICICI Pru & LIC (insurance), and AMC majors.
- **Regulators.** RBI (NBFCs), SEBI (markets), IRDAI (insurance).
- **KPIs.** AUM growth, NIM, credit cost/GNPA (lending), combined ratio (insurance), expense ratio, cross-sell.
- **Case tension.** Under-penetration = growth, but the swing factor is **credit cost and distribution efficiency**; digital acquisition and account-aggregator rails are reshaping economics.

## Banking
- **Snapshot.** Deposits of ~₹262 lakh crore (~$3.0T) and credit of ~₹214 lakh crore at end-FY26 (31 Mar 2026). Public- and private-sector banks together held ~₹287 lakh crore (~$3.4T) of assets in FY25, with public-sector banks at ~60% of that. India runs a world-leading digital-payments stack: UPI carries ~49% of global **real-time** payment volume (2023 data) and is the world's largest retail fast-payment system by volume. An older "~$2.7T assets; ~40% of global digital transactions" line is stale on both counts (see [Payments](#payments)).
- **Structure.** Scheduled commercial banks (public, private, foreign) + small-finance/payments banks + a large fintech overlay (~$150B).
- **KPIs.** NIM, CASA ratio, cost-to-income, GNPA/NNPA, credit growth, CET1/capital adequacy, RoA/RoE.
- **Regulator.** RBI (CBDC pilot, Retail Direct, account-aggregator framework in play).
- **Size & growth.** Say which measure: deposits (liability side), credit (asset side) or total balance-sheet assets. They are not interchangeable, because assets also include investments, cash and reserves funded by capital and borrowings. FY26: credit +16%, deposits +13.4%, so the credit-deposit ratio rose to ~81%. Health (FY25): GNPA ~2.2% (Mar 2025), a multi-decade low; RoA ~1.4%, RoE ~13.5%. 12 public-sector banks after consolidation.
- **Five forces.** Entry M (licences for universal, small-finance and payments banks, plus capital; fintechs enter through co-lending and partnerships). Suppliers M-H (depositors are the suppliers of funds; when credit outgrows deposits, banks bid up deposit rates and CASA becomes the scarce input). Buyers M-H (large corporates tap bond markets; retail borrowers compare rates digitally). Substitutes H (NBFCs and fintech lenders for credit; mutual funds and market-linked products for savings).
- **Cost vs revenue drivers.** Cost: cost of funds (set by the CASA share), credit provisions, technology and cyber security, compliance (Basel III capital and liquidity), branches and staff. Revenue: credit demand × NIM (net interest income is the outcome, not the driver), fees (transaction banking, cards, wealth and insurance distribution), treasury, recoveries.
- **Key terms.** CASA, NIM, credit-deposit ratio, GNPA/NNPA, provision coverage ratio, CRAR/CET1, Basel III, CRR/SLR, priority-sector lending, securitisation and co-lending, core banking system, regulatory sandbox, prudential norms, account aggregator.
- **Case tension.** Private banks taking share from PSBs; monetizing UPI's zero-MDR funnel via cross-sell to lending; asset quality through the cycle.

## FMCG
- **Snapshot.** ~$110B; famed for deep rural distribution. Segments: food & beverage, home care, personal care, healthcare. E-commerce/D2C is the fastest channel (~28% CAGR).
- **Leaders.** HUL, ITC, Nestlé India, Britannia, Dabur; a wave of D2C challengers in personal care and F&B.
- **KPIs.** Volume vs. value growth, gross margin, A&P spend %, distribution reach (outlets), rural/urban mix, direct-reach %.
- **Formulas.** Machine uptime = run time ÷ planned production time. New-product ROI = net profit from new products ÷ development and launch cost. Customer-service average handle time = total handling time ÷ calls.
- **Case tension.** Premiumization and D2C disruption vs. mass rural distribution moat; input-cost (palm oil, crude derivatives) pass-through; the general-trade→modern-trade→quick-commerce channel shift.

## Iron & steel
- **Snapshot.** India is the world's 2nd-largest crude-steel producer. Products split flat (auto, appliances) vs. long (construction). Coking coal (largely imported) is the input swing factor.
- **Leaders.** JSW Steel, Tata Steel, SAIL, ArcelorMittal Nippon Steel (AM/NS), Jindal Steel & Power; Vedanta in adjacent metals.
- **KPIs.** Crude-steel capacity/utilization, realization/tonne, EBITDA/tonne, coking-coal cost, capacity-expansion pipeline.
- **Case tension.** A global-price-cycle taker with heavy fixed costs; the levers are cost position (captive iron ore, energy), integration, and disciplined capacity build. Green-steel transition is the long-horizon theme.

## IT / ITES
- **Snapshot.** ~$315B of technology-industry revenue in FY26 (estimate), ~$246B of it exported; India is the world's #1 offshoring/services hub. Segments: IT services, BPM/ITES, engineering R&D, product/GCCs.
- **Leaders.** TCS, Infosys, Wipro, HCLTech, Tech Mahindra; plus a booming global-capability-center (GCC) footprint.
- **KPIs.** Revenue growth (CC), EBIT margin, utilization, attrition, deal TCV/book-to-bill, revenue per employee, offshore mix.
- **Size & growth.** Metric: industry-body technology-industry revenue (IT services + BPM + ER&D + software products + hardware; domestic + export). ~$315B in FY26 (a February 2026 estimate, not audited), +6.1% YoY: IT services ~$149B, ER&D ~$63B, BPM ~$59B, products ~$23B, hardware ~$21B. Exports ~$246B (~78%). Headcount ~6M, +2.3% (~135k net adds). Revenue grew ~2.7× faster than headcount, so the non-linear, productivity-led model is showing up in the numbers. AI-linked revenue ~$10–12B. "IT services" (~$149B) and "tech industry" (~$315B) are different measures, so name the one you use.
- **Five forces.** Entry M (low for boutiques and AI-native firms; high for large multi-year deals that need scale and references). Suppliers M (skilled talent is the input, especially in AI, cloud and cyber; attrition sets wage inflation). Buyers H (global clients consolidate vendors, rebid contracts and demand productivity pass-backs). Substitutes M-H (client-owned GCCs insourcing work, GenAI automating code and BPM tasks, SaaS replacing custom builds).
- **Cost vs revenue drivers.** Cost: employee cost (the dominant line), subcontractors, onsite travel and visas, SG&A, facilities. Revenue: global discretionary tech spend (US/Europe BFSI, retail), deal TCV and renewals, cloud/data/AI programmes, pricing model (fixed-price productivity vs T&M), GCC build-operate-transfer deals.
- **Key terms.** TCV/ACV, book-to-bill, utilisation, pyramid, offshore mix, onsite-offshore model, GCC, build-operate-transfer, ER&D, BPM, nearshoring, constant currency, fixed-price vs time-and-materials. Use utilisation and pyramid economics, not SaaS metrics like ARR/MRR, for a services business. GCCs are both a revenue pool and a substitute.
- **Case tension.** Wage inflation and attrition vs. margin; dependence on discretionary client spend (US/Europe); and the **GenAI disruption** to the linear headcount-growth model — the pyramid-to-productivity shift.

## Metals & mining
- **Snapshot.** A top global producer of aluminium and zinc; also copper, iron ore, and coal. Highly concentrated and capital/energy-intensive.
- **Leaders.** Hindalco/Novelis (aluminium, Aditya Birla), Vedanta/Hindustan Zinc, NALCO, Coal India (coal monopoly), NMDC (iron ore).
- **KPIs.** Cost of production/tonne (cost-curve position), LME-linked realization, captive power/ore integration, capacity utilization.
- **Case tension.** LME-price takers where survival is set by **cost-curve position**; captive power and vertical integration are the differentiators; ESG/decarbonization pressure on smelting.

## Oil & gas
- **Snapshot.** India is the 3rd-largest oil consumer (~5.6 mb/d in 2024) and imports ~89% of its crude requirement (88.7% in FY26, provisional, a record high); the 4th-largest LNG importer. Chain: upstream (E&P) → midstream (storage/transport) → downstream (refining/retail).
- **Leaders.** ONGC (#1 upstream), Indian Oil / BPCL / HPCL (downstream PSUs), Reliance (integrated, Jamnagar), GAIL (gas).
- **KPIs.** Gross refining margin (GRM), refinery utilization, upstream reserves/production, marketing margin, import dependence.
- **Formulas.** Reserve life (years) = remaining reserves ÷ annual production. Unit production cost = (fixed + variable costs) ÷ units produced (e.g. per barrel).
- **Size & growth.** No single "market size" fits this sector, so use physical measures. Crude import dependence was 88.7% in FY26 (provisional), up from 85.5% in FY22 as domestic output declines. Crude imports were ~245.8 MMT in FY26 (+1%). Refining capacity is ~258 MMTPA (4th-largest globally), targeted at ~310 MMTPA by 2030. India is a net exporter of products (~61 MMT exported in FY26). Import-bill sanity check: 245.8 MMT × ~7.3 barrels per tonne × ~$70/bbl ≈ **$125B** [ILLUSTRATIVE; it moves one-for-one with crude price]. That is why a slide quoting a "~$40B oil & gas market" cannot be the whole sector.
- **Five forces.** Entry L (licensing rounds exist, but geology, capital and PSU-dominated fuel retail with administered pricing deter entry). Suppliers H (crude is priced globally, and sourcing is exposed to OPEC+ decisions and sanctions). Buyers M-H (the government shapes retail fuel and gas prices; industrial buyers switch fuels). Substitutes M-H (EVs in 2W/3W, CNG, ethanol blending; renewables and green hydrogen over the long run).
- **Cost vs revenue drivers.** Cost: crude feedstock (dominant), lifting cost (upstream), refinery energy and O&M, freight and insurance, depreciation and finance. Revenue: GRM and crack spreads, marketing margin on fuels, **government compensation for under-recoveries** (e.g. LPG) and **administered gas-price indexation**, petrochemicals, product exports. Downstream revenue therefore depends partly on policy, not only on spreads.
- **Key terms.** GRM, crack spread, lifting cost, reserve replacement ratio, P50/P90 reserves, operating netback, under-recovery, administered (APM) gas, city gas distribution (CGD), OALP/HELP licensing, E20 ethanol blending, strategic petroleum reserve, SAF, MMTPA.
- **Case tension.** Import-price and geopolitics exposure (crude sourcing diversified toward Russia/Guyana); refining-margin cyclicality; and the capital pivot into the energy transition (green hydrogen, biofuels).

## Power
- **Snapshot.** ~411GW installed capacity vs. ~205GW peak demand; India is the 3rd-largest producer/consumer. Mix ~57% thermal, ~30% renewables, plus hydro/nuclear. National target ~500GW non-fossil.
- **Leaders.** NTPC (largest generator), Adani Power, Tata Power, JSW Energy; renewables led by Adani Green, ReNew, Tata Power.
- **Chain.** Generation → transmission → distribution (discoms, the stressed link) → retail. Governed by the Electricity Act, 2003; regulated by CERC/SERCs; PPAs set economics.
- **KPIs.** PLF (plant load factor), tariff (₹/kWh), AT&C losses, discom receivable days, renewable capacity adds.
- **Case tension.** Renewables shift + discom financial distress (receivables, AT&C losses) are the twin themes; PPA structure and grid integration drive returns.

## Telecommunication
- **Snapshot.** Gross revenue of ~₹4.2 lakh crore (~$48B) a year at the Q4 FY26 run-rate, and ~1.35B subscribers (Jul 2026); world's 2nd-largest subscriber and internet base. A consolidated **3-player** market after brutal price wars. Segments: mobile, fixed-line/broadband, enterprise.
- **Leaders.** Jio (Reliance), Bharti Airtel, Vodafone Idea (financially stressed) + BSNL.
- **KPIs.** ARPU (the key repair metric), subscriber net adds, data usage/sub, spectrum + capex intensity, churn.
- **Size & growth.** Metric (regulator data): sector gross revenue ₹1,05,118 crore in Q4 FY26 (+6.9% YoY) and adjusted gross revenue (AGR) ₹86,716 crore (+9.5%). Annualised, that is ~₹4.2 lakh crore gross (~$48B) and ~₹3.5 lakh crore AGR (~$39B). The older "~$37B" was close to an AGR-type measure, so state which one you mean. Subscribers: 1.354B (Jul 2026), overall tele-density ~95%. Wireless share (Jul 2026): Jio ~39.3%, Airtel ~38.0% and gaining, Vodafone Idea ~15.5%, BSNL ~7.2%. Blended wireless ARPU ~₹196/month (Q4 FY26, +7% YoY). 5G fixed-wireless subscribers ~13M.
- **Five forces.** Entry L (spectrum auctions, network capex and the fate of past entrants). Suppliers M (few network-equipment vendors, tower companies, and the government as spectrum seller and collector of AGR-linked levies). Buyers H (prepaid plans and number portability keep switching cost low, though operators match tariff moves). Substitutes L-M (OTT messaging and calling replace voice/SMS revenue but ride on data; satellite broadband at the edges).
- **Cost vs revenue drivers.** Cost: spectrum (auction payments, amortisation and interest), network capex and opex (tower energy is a large line), licence fee and spectrum-usage charges levied on AGR, dealer commissions, customer care. Revenue: subscribers × ARPU (tariff repair, 4G→5G upgrades, postpaid mix), fixed wireless and FTTH home broadband, enterprise, bundled digital services.
- **Key terms.** ARPU, AGR (the base for licence fees and the subject of the long-running dues dispute), tele-density (connections per 100 people: **above 100% in cities means multi-SIM users, not more users than people**; the highest circle is >300% and the lowest ~64%), MNP, MOU, FWA, FTTH, spectrum bands, tower tenancy ratio, 5G SA/NSA.
- **Case tension.** ARPU repair after tariff wars, heavy 5G/spectrum capex, and the 3-player market's stability (Vodafone Idea's viability). Monetizing 5G and the digital-services layer on top of connectivity is the growth question.

## Healthcare (hospitals)
- **Snapshot.** A large, fast-growing hospital market driven by rising chronic disease, an ageing population, insurance and government-scheme coverage, and medical tourism. Telemedicine, diagnostics and health-tech attract steady PE/VC money.
- **Leaders.** Apollo Hospitals, Max Healthcare, Fortis, Manipal, Narayana Health, Aster DM; plus large trust/charitable and government systems.
- **Value chain (patient journey).** Awareness/referral → OPD consult and diagnostics → admission (IPD) → surgical or medical treatment → discharge, billing and insurance claim → follow-up.
- **KPIs.** Bed occupancy, ALOS, ARPOB, payer mix (cash / private insurance / government schemes), staff-to-patient ratio, mortality and readmission rates, drug and consumable cost per stay.
- **Formulas.**
  - Occupancy = occupied bed-days ÷ available bed-days.
  - ALOS = inpatient days ÷ discharges.
  - ARPOB = inpatient revenue ÷ occupied bed-days.
  - Bed turnover = discharges ÷ beds; per year it equals occupancy × 365 ÷ ALOS (e.g. 70% × 365 ÷ 4 days ≈ 64 patients per bed a year [ILLUSTRATIVE]).
- **Size & growth.** Metric: hospital-services revenue, a research-firm estimate. ~$122B in 2025, rising to ~$202B by 2030 (~10.6% CAGR over five years). Other firms' 2025 estimates range from ~$105B to ~$193B, so quote a range and name the definition. An old "healthcare ~$638B by 2025" projection is not an actual; avoid it. Financing context (national health accounts, FY23): government health spending ~1.4% of GDP; out-of-pocket spending ~43% of total health expenditure (up from ~39% in FY22 after a long decline from ~64% in FY14); private insurance ~9% of total health spending.
- **Five forces.** Entry L (capex, land, doctor recruitment, accreditation and a multi-year ramp to occupancy). Suppliers M-H (specialist doctors bring patients with them, so doctor retention is a P&L lever; device and consumable makers). Buyers M overall: low for cash patients in urgent and tertiary care, **high for government schemes and large insurers** that set package rates. Substitutes L (telemedicine and day-care shift some volume; nothing replaces tertiary care).
- **Cost vs revenue drivers.** Cost: doctor and nurse pay (the largest line), drugs and consumables, equipment capex and depreciation, facility operations, compliance. Revenue: occupancy × ARPOB, payer mix (insured and international patients lift ARPOB), specialty mix (oncology, cardiac sciences, transplants), diagnostics, medical value travel, tier-2/3 expansion.
- **Key terms.** ARPOB, ALOS, bed turnover, doctor utilisation, payer mix, PM-JAY (government health-insurance scheme) and package rates, cashless claims and TPAs, NABH, brownfield vs greenfield beds, medical value travel.
- **Case tension.** A high-fixed-cost business that turns on **occupancy × ARPOB**. Government-scheme pricing squeezes realisation; doctor retention and brownfield bed additions drive growth. See Drills F and N in `references/india-guesstimates-and-cases.md`.

## Retail
- **Snapshot.** Roughly a trillion-dollar market (~$1.06T in 2024) in which roughly four-fifths of sales are still unorganised (~13M kiranas and other general-trade stores). Estimates of the unorganised share run from ~75% to ~88% depending on the base year and on whether e-commerce counts as organised; the older "~90%" is dated. Formats span hypermarkets, value retail, specialty, fashion, omnichannel and quick-commerce. FDI allows 100% in single-brand retail and up to 51% in multi-brand retail.
- **Leaders.** Reliance Retail, Avenue Supermarts (DMart), Trent, Aditya Birla Fashion & Retail, Shoppers Stop.
- **Value chain.** Sourcing and private-label development → warehousing and inventory → distribution → store or online → customer and loyalty.
- **KPIs.** Like-for-like (same-store) growth, sales per sq ft, average transaction value, gross margin, inventory turnover, GMROI, shrinkage, private-label share.
- **Formulas.**
  - Average transaction value = sales ÷ number of transactions.
  - Inventory turnover = cost of goods sold ÷ average inventory.
  - GMROI = gross margin ÷ average inventory cost.
- **Size & growth.** Metric: total retail sales across all channels (kirana, modern trade, e-commerce), USD, calendar year. ~$1.06T in 2024 and ~$1.09–1.12T in 2025; projected ~$1.93T by 2030, which is ~10.5% CAGR over six years. A ">$2.36T by 2030" projection implies ~16–17% a year from 2025, so treat it as an upper bound. E-commerce is ~7% of retail (~$75B, 2024), heading to ~13–14% (~$260B) by 2030. The top retailers together hold under 5% of the market. Global rank: not verified, so do not quote one.
- **Five forces.** Entry M (a format is easy to open and hard to scale; real estate and supply chain are the gates). Suppliers L-M (fragmented suppliers facing scaled retailers; large FMCG brands still hold leverage). Buyers H (low switching cost, price- and promotion-driven). Substitutes H (e-commerce and quick commerce; kiranas' credit and home delivery).
- **Cost vs revenue drivers.** Cost: rent (the swing line), staff, inventory holding, shrinkage and fresh spoilage, logistics, discounting. Revenue: store count × sales per sq ft (throughput), like-for-like growth, category mix and private label, omnichannel, loyalty. Store-level contribution (rent vs throughput) is the unit that decides expansion.
- **Key terms.** LFL/SSSG, sales per sq ft, footfall conversion, basket size/ATV, GMROI, shrinkage, stock-out rate, inventory days, private label, store-level contribution, general trade vs modern trade, hypermarket vs value retail, omnichannel.
- **Case tension.** Store economics (rent vs. throughput), the value-retail vs. quick-commerce squeeze on high-frequency trips, and private label as the margin lever. Disposable income and store location drive demand.

## Hospitality
- **Snapshot.** Travel and tourism contribute roughly 5% of GDP and a larger share of jobs, and hospitality is a meaningful forex earner. Demand is cyclical (it collapsed in 2020–21), with weddings/MICE and domestic leisure as structural drivers.
- **Leaders.** IHCL (Taj), EIH (Oberoi), ITC Hotels, Lemon Tree, Chalet; plus budget aggregators and midscale franchise brands.
- **Value chain.** Procurement → rooms and housekeeping → F&B → events and recreation → sales and distribution (direct, OTA, corporate) → guest services and loyalty.
- **KPIs.** Occupancy, ADR (ARR), RevPAR, GOPPAR, F&B share of revenue, direct-booking share, staff per room.
- **Formulas.**
  - Occupancy = rooms sold ÷ rooms available.
  - ADR = room revenue ÷ rooms sold.
  - **RevPAR = ADR × occupancy = room revenue ÷ rooms available.**
  - Emphasis by segment: luxury protects ADR, midscale chases occupancy, economy is judged on RevPAR.
- **Case tension.** Asset-heavy ownership vs. asset-light management contracts; utilisation × yield (see Drill F in `references/india-guesstimates-and-cases.md`); new supply in hot micro-markets. See `references/hospitality-travel-leisure.md` for global depth.

## Logistics
- **Snapshot.** A ~$160B+ sector that employs millions. Trucking is highly fragmented, while organised express, 3PL and warehousing players scale with e-commerce. Logistics cost is widely cited at ~13–14% of GDP; a 2023 government-commissioned estimate put it nearer ~8–9%. **Define the measure before quoting either.** The National Logistics Policy, GST e-way bills and dedicated freight corridors are the structural shifts.
- **Leaders.** Delhivery, Blue Dart, TCI, Allcargo, CONCOR (rail containers), Mahindra Logistics.
- **Value chain.** Inbound → warehousing → line-haul → sortation/hubs → last mile → reverse logistics (returns). Support activities: technology (visibility, route optimisation), workforce training, infrastructure (roads, ports, airports).
- **KPIs.** Capacity utilisation, fill rate, on-time-in-full, perfect-order rate, cost per shipment/kg, first-attempt delivery success, e-commerce RTO rate, inventory turnover.
- **Formulas.**
  - Capacity utilisation = actual weight carried ÷ maximum possible weight × 100.
  - Order accuracy = orders delivered on time without errors ÷ total orders × 100.
  - Inventory turnover = net sales ÷ average inventory at selling price.
- **Case tension.** Empty backhauls and fragmentation depress truck utilisation; last-mile cost dominates e-commerce unit economics; rail vs. road modal mix; integrated 3PL vs. asset-light brokerage.

## Defence manufacturing
- **Snapshot.** A state-shaped market. One ministry buys through tenders, which makes it a monopsony, and indigenisation rules steer that spend to domestic suppliers. Defence PSUs still make most of the output, but private firms now earn ~45% of export revenue. Segments: aerospace, naval shipbuilding, land systems, missiles and ammunition, defence electronics (radars, EW, avionics). Buyer and regulator: the Ministry of Defence (Department of Defence Production), with DRDO as the state R&D arm.
- **Size & growth.** Metric: value of domestic defence production (DPSUs, other PSUs and private industry, ₹). ~₹1.78 lakh crore (~$20B) in FY26, ~+18% YoY (FY25: ~₹1.51 lakh crore), and about 3× FY15, i.e. ~10–11% CAGR over 11 years. Exports: ₹38,424 crore (~$4.4B) in FY26, +63% YoY (FY25: ₹23,622 crore). That is up from ₹686 crore in FY14, ~40% CAGR over 12 years. DPSUs earned ~55% and private firms ~45% of FY26 exports, across 80+ destination countries. Stated goals of ~₹3 lakh crore of production and ~₹50,000 crore of exports by 2029 [verify before quoting] need ~19% and ~9% a year from FY26, so the production goal needs the recent pace to hold. Third-party "defence market" figures (budget-based or procurement-based) measure something else, so name the metric.
- **Leaders.** DPSUs: HAL (aircraft, helicopters), BEL (defence electronics), BDL (missiles), Mazagon Dock, GRSE and Cochin Shipyard (warships), BEML (land systems). Private: L&T, Tata Advanced Systems, Bharat Forge (Kalyani group), Adani Defence, Solar Industries (explosives and ammunition).
- **Value chain.** R&D and system design (state R&D labs, innovation grants to start-ups, private R&D) → Tier-1 primes and integrators (aircraft, ships, missiles) → Tier-2 sub-systems (radars, avionics, sensors, EW, propulsion) → Tier-3/4 components (thousands of MSMEs) → MRO and life-cycle support across decades of platform life.
- **KPIs.** Indigenous content ratio, on-time delivery against contract milestones, production lead time, field failure rate, order-book cover, contract win rate, export share of revenue.
- **Formulas.**
  - Indigenous content = domestic value added ÷ contract value.
  - Order-book cover (years) = order book ÷ annual revenue (e.g. ₹90,000 crore ÷ ₹30,000 crore = 3 years of visible revenue [ILLUSTRATIVE]).
- **Five forces.** Entry L-M (qualification, security clearance and long cycles; indigenisation lists and FDI liberalisation are lowering it). Suppliers M-H (critical minerals, semiconductors, engines and some sub-systems are imported from a few countries). Buyers **H** (a single ministry buying through tenders with lowest-bidder evaluation). Substitutes L (nothing replaces core platforms). **Error fixed:** rating substitutes High because "the mix is shifting to drones and asymmetric systems" → Low. A shift inside defence spending is technology change within the industry, while a substitute meets the same need from outside it.
- **Cost vs revenue drivers.** Cost: imported raw materials and sub-systems, currency (USD/EUR-priced imports), customs duty, localisation development cost, long working-capital cycles. Revenue: the capital-acquisition budget, domestic-procurement mandates, modernisation and geopolitics, exports, FDI-enabled joint ventures.
- **Key terms.** DPSU, indigenous content (IC), positive indigenisation lists, Defence Acquisition Procedure (DAP) and its buy categories, L1 (lowest-bidder) award, offsets, iDEX (innovation grants), Tier-1/2/3, MRO, strategic-partnership model.
- **Case tension.** With a monopsony buyer and mandated indigenous content, the winning lever is **qualifying as a Tier-1 or Tier-2 domestic supplier** and holding delivery milestones, not price. The binding constraints are import-dependent inputs and multi-year procurement cycles. Trap: applying consumer-market Porter logic, or US GovCon set-aside logic, to a single-ministry tender market (for the US contrast, see `references/public-sector-government-defense.md`).

## Ed-tech
- **Snapshot.** A post-boom reset. Pandemic-era hypergrowth was funded by venture capital; funding then fell ~95%, from ~$4.1–4.3B (2021) to ~$215M (2024), and the pandemic-era category leader, valued at ~$22B in 2022, ended up in insolvency proceedings. Survivors went hybrid (online plus offline centres), led by test prep, and the sector is consolidating. Segments: K-12 supplementary learning, test prep (engineering, medical and government exams), higher education and upskilling, school SaaS/B2B. Oversight: Ministry of Education; 2024 guidelines for coaching centres.
- **Size & growth.** No official statistic exists, so define the scope first. Estimates for 2025 run from ~$3.6B (online ed-tech only) to ~$7.5B (including hybrid test prep, B2B and upskilling); online direct-to-consumer K-12 is only ~$2–3B [low confidence; verify before quoting]. Forecasts of ~25–28% CAGR (e.g. ~$3.6B → ~$33B by 2034) are research-firm upside cases, not trend. Do not quote a 2030–34 forecast as current size, and do not treat a company valuation as market size.
- **Leaders.** PhysicsWallah (hybrid test prep; listed Nov 2025), upGrad and Eruditus/Great Learning (upskilling and higher education), Unacademy and Vedantu (both downsized), LEAD and Teachmint (school SaaS).
- **Value chain.** Content development (faculty, curriculum, accreditation) → platform and technology (LMS, AI personalisation, data security) → learner acquisition (free-to-paid funnel, counsellor-led sales, enterprise B2B) → delivery and engagement (live and recorded classes, doubt-solving, mentorship, offline centres) → monetisation (subscriptions, course fees, certification, enterprise contracts).
- **KPIs.** Free-to-paid conversion, CAC and CAC payback, LTV/CAC, completion rate, renewal/retention, engagement minutes per learner, refund rate, revenue per offline centre, faculty attrition.
- **Formulas.**
  - Paid conversion = paying learners ÷ registered (free) learners.
  - LTV = annual revenue per paying learner × gross margin × expected paid years. Example: ₹15,000 × 60% × 1.5 years = ₹13,500; against a CAC of ₹9,000, LTV/CAC = 1.5, well below the ~3× a sustainable consumer subscription needs [ILLUSTRATIVE]. The bust was this ratio, subsidised by venture money.
- **Five forces.** Entry M-H (content is cheap to make, and educators can build audiences on free video first). Suppliers M-H (star faculty carry learners with them, so faculty exits move cohorts). Buyers H (price-sensitive parents, free alternatives, refund pressure). Substitutes H (offline coaching, free video, school itself).
- **Cost vs revenue drivers.** Cost: CAC (performance marketing, counsellor sales teams), faculty pay, content production, cloud and technology, offline-centre rent. Revenue: paid conversion × price, renewals, hybrid centres (higher willingness to pay and better retention), vernacular content for tier-2/3 learners, enterprise upskilling contracts.
- **Key terms.** Free-to-paid funnel, CAC payback, MOOC, LMS, adaptive learning, blended (hybrid) learning, completion rate, learning analytics, accreditation and certification, test prep.
- **Case tension.** The reset was a **CAC/LTV problem**: growth was bought with marketing at unit economics that never closed. The live question is whether the hybrid model lifts retention and price enough to pay back CAC. Trap: extrapolating pandemic-era adoption as structural demand. For the US school-buyer (B2B) lens, see `references/industry-intelligence.md` (Education).

## Electric vehicles (four-wheelers)
- **Snapshot.** An early-adoption market. Electric cars were ~4.3% of new car (PV) sales in FY26, up from ~2.7% in FY25 and ~0.5% in FY22. Adoption is gated by total cost of ownership (TCO), charger density, battery cost and resale values, not by sticker price alone. Policy: PM E-DRIVE (the successor to FAME-II), PLI for auto components and advanced-chemistry cells (ACC), and an import-duty concession for makers that commit to local investment. The September 2025 GST cut on ICE cars narrowed the EV price gap (EVs were already at 5% GST). Regulators: Ministry of Heavy Industries (schemes), MoRTH.
- **Size & growth.** Metric: retail registrations (national vehicle-registration data). Electric PVs: ~1.98 lakh units in FY26, +84% YoY, ~4.3% of ~4.6M PV registrations; monthly share has since run at ~5–6%. All EVs: ~2.45M in FY26, +25%, ~8.3% of all vehicles, mostly e-2W (1.40M, 57%) and e-3W (0.83M, 34%); e-PVs are only ~8% of EV units. So an "8% EV penetration" headline is a 2W/3W number, and the car figure is ~4%. No verified value measure exists. Rough value check: ~1.98 lakh e-cars × ~₹15 lakh average price ≈ ₹29,700 crore ≈ **$3.4B** [ILLUSTRATIVE], so a "$14B EV market" must include two- and three-wheelers and buses.
- **Leaders.** FY26 e-PV share: Tata Motors ~39%, JSW MG Motor ~26%, Mahindra ~21% (top three ~87%); then Hyundai, BYD, BMW, Kia, Maruti Suzuki (e-Vitara), VinFast and Tesla (entered 2025).
- **Value chain.** Cells and materials (largely imported; PLI-ACC for local cells) → battery pack and powertrain (pack ~30–40% of vehicle cost [ILLUSTRATIVE]; BMS) → vehicle assembly and homologation (incentive eligibility tied to localisation) → distribution (dealers, D2C, fleets) → charging and after-sales (public and home charging, OTA updates, battery second life and recycling).
- **KPIs.** Battery cost ($/kWh), TCO gap vs ICE (₹/km), EVs per public charger, charger utilisation, localisation ratio, real-world range, residual value, fleet share of sales.
- **Formulas.**
  - TCO per km = (purchase price − resale value + financing cost) ÷ lifetime km + energy ₹/km + maintenance ₹/km.
  - Breakeven km = EV price premium ÷ (ICE running cost per km − EV running cost per km). Example: ₹3 lakh premium ÷ (₹7.0/km petrol − ₹1.5/km electricity) ≈ 54,500 km. That is ~4.5 years for a private car at 12,000 km a year, but under one year for a fleet cab at 60,000 km [ILLUSTRATIVE]. This is why fleets electrify first.
- **Five forces.** Entry M (global EV brands enter through duty concessions, but scale, service networks and PLI thresholds raise the bar). Suppliers **H** (cells and critical minerals come from a concentrated, largely imported base). Buyers M-H (price-sensitive, range-anxious and unsure about resale; fleets negotiate hard). Substitutes M (ICE, CNG and strong hybrids, which the 2025 GST cut on ICE cars made cheaper).
- **Cost vs revenue drivers.** Cost: the battery (the single largest line), imported cells and minerals, charging capex, platform R&D, the localisation gap. Revenue: fleet electrification (high-km users), state and urban incentives, after-sales and software, charging monetisation, exports.
- **Key terms.** BEV, PHEV, strong hybrid, BMS, ACC (advanced-chemistry cell), PM E-DRIVE (successor to FAME-II), localisation ratio, homologation, TCO, V2G, battery swapping.
- **Case tension.** Value is captured at the **cell**, where India is import-dependent, and adoption follows TCO and charger density. Trap: using an all-EV size or penetration figure (~8%) for a four-wheeler question (~4%). For a two-wheeler entry case, see Drill J in `references/india-guesstimates-and-cases.md`.

## Food & beverage processing
- **Snapshot.** The processing layer between farm and shelf: dairy, grain milling, edible oils, meat and fish, fruit and vegetable processing, beverages, and ready-to-eat/ready-to-cook foods. It is distinct from packaged FMCG (see [FMCG](#fmcg)). The sector is highly fragmented with a large unorganised base, yet it is the largest employer in registered manufacturing (~12.8% of jobs, 2023–24). Processed foods rose from ~13.7% of agri-exports (FY15) to ~20.4% (FY25). Regulators: Ministry of Food Processing Industries (MoFPI, including PLI), FSSAI (food safety and labelling).
- **Size & growth.** Two measures that must not be mixed. (a) Gross value added (GVA) at constant prices: ~₹2.24 lakh crore (~$27B) in FY24, up from ~₹1.34 lakh crore in FY15, about 7.9% of manufacturing GVA. The official "~6.6% average annual growth" is an average of yearly rates; the compound rate from the endpoints is ~5.9% over nine years (averaging yearly rates overstates growth when growth is volatile). (b) Gross processed-food market: ~₹30.5 lakh crore (~$355B) in 2024, a research estimate of output value, not value added. Neither is the packaged-FMCG market (~$110B).
- **Leaders.** Dairy co-operatives (Amul/GCMMF, Mother Dairy); staples and edible oils (AWL Agri Business, Tata Consumer); packaged foods (Nestlé India, Britannia, ITC Foods, Parle); snacks and sweets (Haldiram's); plus a long tail of regional and D2C brands.
- **Value chain.** Sourcing (from fragmented mandi buying toward direct sourcing through FPOs and contract farming) → primary processing (milling, chilling, crushing) → secondary processing and packaging (retort, IQF, aseptic) → cold chain and distribution → retail: kirana for reach, modern trade for visibility, quick commerce for impulse.
- **KPIs.** Yield (recovery) rate, OEE, seasonal capacity utilisation, wastage/spoilage %, raw-material inventory days, labour cost %, complaints per million units, recall rate.
- **Formulas.**
  - Yield = saleable output ÷ raw input (e.g. 100 kg of paddy → ~65–67 kg of milled rice).
  - OEE = availability × performance × quality (e.g. 0.85 × 0.90 × 0.98 ≈ 0.75 [ILLUSTRATIVE]).
- **Five forces.** Entry M-H (low-capex categories, D2C brands and contract manufacturing). Suppliers M (farmers are individually weak, but monsoon-driven commodity swings pass straight through; FPOs are building bargaining power). Buyers H (modern trade and quick-commerce platforms dictate margins and shelf space). Substitutes H (fresh, unprocessed and home-cooked food).
- **Cost vs revenue drivers.** Cost: agri-commodity prices, cold chain, utilisation of seasonal plants, packaging, testing, compliance and recalls, automation capex vs labour. Revenue: urbanisation and working households (RTE/RTC), rural reach, pack-size and price-point strategy, category expansion, modern trade, e-commerce and quick commerce, exports.
- **Key terms.** FPO, IQF, retort packaging, RTE/RTC/RTS, clean label, front-of-pack labelling, SKU rationalisation, farm-to-fork traceability, OEE, PLI for food processing.
- **Case tension.** Margin is made at **yield and utilisation** (seasonal, perishable inputs), and one brand now needs three shelf strategies (kirana reach, modern-trade image, quick-commerce impulse), each with its own pack sizes and margins. Front-of-pack labelling may force reformulation. Trap: using the ~$355B gross market or the GVA figure as the packaged-FMCG market.

## Food delivery
- **Snapshot.** A two-player market: Zomato (Eternal) and Swiggy hold more than 90%. It is an asset-light marketplace protected by two-sided network effects (restaurants, riders, customers). Online delivery is ~11% of India's ~₹7 trillion food-services market, projected at ~18% by FY31. Both leaders are now EBITDA-positive in food delivery, at an adjusted ~3–6% of order value (Q4 FY26). Oversight: FSSAI (restaurant hygiene), the competition regulator (platform conduct), and new social-security rules for gig workers.
- **Size & growth.** Metric: gross order value (GOV) of platform-delivered food. ~₹79,000 crore (~$9B) in FY25, up from ~₹27,000 crore in FY20 (~24% CAGR over five years); projected ~₹1.7–2.2 lakh crore by FY30 (~17–23% CAGR). The leaders' food businesses grew ~17–20% YoY in Q1 FY27. One leader reports net order value (NOV) and the other GOV, so share arithmetic on reported numbers is approximate.
- **Leaders.** Zomato (Eternal) with a mid-50s % share and Swiggy with a mid-40s % share (the split is an estimate); the rest is restaurant-owned ordering and open-network apps.
- **Value chain.** Restaurant and rider onboarding → customer acquisition and discovery (search, ads, loyalty programmes) → order processing (restaurant prep, dispatch timing) → last-mile delivery (batching, routing, service windows) → monetisation (commission, delivery and platform fees, restaurant ads, subscriptions).
- **KPIs.** GOV, orders per day, AOV, take rate (revenue ÷ GOV), contribution per order, delivery cost per order, rider utilisation (orders per rider-hour), cancellation rate, monthly transacting users, order frequency.
- **Formulas.**
  - Contribution per order = AOV × commission rate + customer fees + ad income per order − delivery cost − discounts − payment and support cost. Example: ₹430 × 20% = ₹86, + ₹15 fees + ₹8 ads = ₹109; − ₹55 delivery − ₹20 discounts − ₹14 payment and support = **₹20 per order, ~4.7% of AOV** [ILLUSTRATIVE].
  - Reconciliation: orders per day × AOV × 365 = GOV. ₹79,000 crore ÷ (₹430 × 365) ≈ 5.0M orders a day [ILLUSTRATIVE AOV]. **Error fixed:** quoting "~3M orders a day" alongside a "~$26.5B market" → 3M × ₹400 × 365 ≈ ₹43,800 crore ≈ $5B a year, about a fifth of the claimed market (a top-down size and a bottom-up count must reconcile before either is quoted).
- **Five forces.** Entry **L** despite asset-light economics (rider density and network effects give incumbents a structurally lower cost per order). Suppliers M (restaurants depend on the platforms, but large chains push their own ordering channels; gig-welfare rules raise rider cost). Buyers H (zero switching cost, multi-homing, discount-sensitive). Substitutes M (dine-in, takeaway, home cooking, direct restaurant ordering, ready-to-eat from quick commerce).
- **Cost vs revenue drivers.** Cost: rider cost (the largest line), discounts and CAC, payment-gateway fees, customer support, technology, gig-worker social-security contributions. Revenue: commission, delivery and platform fees, restaurant advertising, subscription programmes, AOV growth.
- **Key terms.** GOV vs NOV, take rate, AOV, batching, cloud (dark) kitchen, platform fee, promoted listings, monthly transacting users, delivery partner, gig-worker social security.
- **Case tension.** Growth vs contribution per order, with restaurants squeezed by commissions and ad fees and rider costs rising with gig-welfare rules. Trap: quoting the market size without reconciling it to orders per day × AOV. See Drills G, H and L in `references/india-guesstimates-and-cases.md`.

## Insurance
- **Snapshot.** Under-penetrated, and the gap is **protection** (term life, health), not savings-linked cover. Penetration (premium ÷ GDP) was 3.7% in FY25 (life 2.7%, non-life 1.0%), down from a 4.2% peak in FY22, and life penetration has now fallen for three straight years. Density (premium per head) was ~$97 (life ~$72, non-life ~$25). India is the 10th-largest insurance market by premium (~1.8% of the global total). FDI in insurance companies is now allowed up to 100%, raised from 74% by a December 2025 amendment that also cut the capital requirement for foreign reinsurers. Regulator: IRDAI.
- **Size & growth.** Metric: total gross premium underwritten (life + non-life). ₹11.93 lakh crore in FY25 (~$140B); claims paid ~₹8.36 lakh crore; AUM ~₹74.4 lakh crore (Mar 2025). FY26: life new-business premium ₹4.60 lakh crore (+15.7%, after +5.1% in FY25); non-life gross direct premium ₹3.36 lakh crore (+9.3%, health-led). Density check: ~$140B ÷ ~1.44B people ≈ $97 per head.
- **Leaders.** Life: LIC (~57% of FY26 first-year premium; total premium income ~₹5.36 lakh crore), then SBI Life, HDFC Life, ICICI Prudential. Non-life: New India Assurance (PSU), ICICI Lombard, the Bajaj group's general insurer, and standalone health insurers such as Star Health.
- **Value chain.** Product design and pricing (actuarial work, underwriting) → distribution (agents, bancassurance, brokers, web aggregators, corporate agents) → policy administration (issuance, renewals, premium collection, servicing) → claims (verification, fraud detection, settlement) → investment of the float, with reinsurance ceding peak risk.
- **KPIs.** Life: persistency (13th- and 61st-month), VNB margin, APE, claim-settlement ratio, solvency ratio. General and health: combined ratio, claims ratio, investment yield on the float. Operations: time to settle, cost per claim, strike rate (policies bound ÷ quotes).
- **Formulas.**
  - Penetration = premium ÷ GDP; density = premium ÷ population.
  - Combined ratio = (claims incurred + operating expenses + commissions) ÷ net earned premium. Example: loss ratio 82% + expense ratio 22% = 104%, an underwriting loss of 4% of premium; investment income on the float of ~8% of premium turns that into a ~4% pre-tax margin [ILLUSTRATIVE].
  - Persistency (month N) = policies (or premium) still in force at month N ÷ those written in the cohort.
  - Solvency ratio = available solvency margin ÷ required margin (regulatory floor 150%).
- **Five forces.** Entry M (100% FDI and lower reinsurer capital invite entrants; licensing and solvency capital still gate). Suppliers M (distributors, especially banks and large agencies, command high commissions; reinsurers price catastrophe and health risk). Buyers M-H (retail buyers price-shop on aggregators; corporate group-health buyers tender hard). Substitutes **L for protection, H for savings-linked life** (mutual funds, deposits, pension products).
- **Cost vs revenue drivers.** Cost: claims (medical inflation in health), commissions and distribution, operations, fraud, solvency capital, compliance. Revenue: new premium (rural, micro-insurance, government-scheme linkages), renewals (persistency), investment income on the float, value-added services (wellness, cashless hospital networks).
- **Key terms.** Premium, penetration vs density, underwriting, claims ratio, combined ratio, solvency ratio, persistency, VNB, APE, bancassurance, reinsurance, float, protection gap, micro-insurance.
- **Case tension.** Distribution cost (bancassurance and agency commissions) and health-claims inflation decide margins, while 100% FDI opens the door to entry and consolidation. Trap: judging an insurer on revenue or EBITDA instead of **combined ratio (general) or persistency and VNB margin (life)**. For insurer economics in depth, see `references/financial-services.md`.

## Media & entertainment
- **Snapshot.** Digital overtook television as the largest segment in 2025. Reach is huge but monetisation is weak: audiences resist paying, advertising funds the majority, and piracy leaks value. Linear-TV ad revenue fell ~10% in 2025 while digital advertising grew ~26%. Regulators: Ministry of Information and Broadcasting, TRAI (broadcast tariffs), CBFC (film certification).
- **Size & growth.** Metric: total industry revenue (advertising + subscription + other, all segments). ₹2.78 trillion (~$32B) in CY2025, +9% YoY; digital crossed ₹1 trillion (~36%). Outlook: ~₹3.3 trillion by 2028, ~6% a year from the 2025 base. Mix (2025): advertising ~₹1.5 trillion (+13%, ~54% of industry revenue), of which digital ~₹947B (~63% of all ads); video subscriptions ~₹163B (+60%) from ~216M paid subscriptions across ~143M households (~1.5 per paying household); filmed entertainment a record ~₹205B; live events +44%; ~40M connected-TV units.
- **Leaders.** Broadcast and streaming: JioStar (the Reliance–Disney combination), Zee, Sony, Sun TV. Cinema exhibition: PVR INOX. Global platforms (YouTube, Meta, Netflix, Prime Video) take much of digital advertising and subscription.
- **Value chain.** Content creation (scripts, production, post-production and VFX) → rights acquisition and programming (licensing, originals, sports rights, libraries) → distribution (TV via DTH and cable, cinema, OTT, social and digital) → monetisation (advertising, subscriptions, tickets and F&B, syndication and licensing) → audience analytics feeding commissioning.
- **KPIs.** Paid subscribers, ARPU, churn, watch time per user, ad yield (revenue per 1,000 impressions or per 10-second slot), TV ratings (TRP/GRP), cinema occupancy, average ticket price (ATP), F&B spend per head (SPH), content cost as % of revenue.
- **Formulas.**
  - Cinema revenue per screen = seats × shows per day × occupancy × (ATP + SPH) × 365. Example: 200 seats × 4 shows × 30% = 240 admits a day × (₹250 + ₹130) = ₹91,200 a day ≈ ₹3.3 crore a year [ILLUSTRATIVE].
  - Subscription revenue = average paid subscribers × monthly ARPU × 12.
- **Five forces.** Entry M (creators and digital-first studios enter cheaply; national distribution and sports rights do not). Suppliers **H** (star talent and marquee sports-rights owners capture the rents in auctions). Buyers H (advertisers shift budgets to measurable digital; viewers are price-sensitive). Substitutes H (short-form social video, gaming, free platforms, piracy).
- **Cost vs revenue drivers.** Cost: content (production, rights, talent fees), distribution and carriage fees, platform technology, marketing, anti-piracy and DRM. Revenue: advertising (the majority), subscriptions (fastest-growing, from a small base), cinema tickets plus F&B, syndication and licensing, live events.
- **Key terms.** AVOD, SVOD, FAST, TRP/GRP, CTV, ATP/SPH, windowing, content amortisation, syndication, DTH, IP rights.
- **Case tension.** India's M&E problem is **monetisation, not reach**: much of digital ad growth goes to global platforms, subscriptions grow from a small base, and content costs (sports rights, stars) inflate faster than ARPU. Trap: using ad-tech funnel metrics (CPC, ROAS, site visits) as the scorecard for a content business. For global economics, see `references/industry-intelligence.md` (Media, Entertainment & Sports); for ticket pricing, Drill M in `references/india-guesstimates-and-cases.md`.

## Payments
- **Snapshot.** UPI is the rails. It carries ~49% of global real-time payment volume (2023 data) and is the world's largest retail fast-payment system by volume. FY26: ~241.6B UPI transactions worth ~₹314 lakh crore, an average ticket of ~₹1,300. August 2026 set a monthly record of ~24.5B transactions (~791M a day; +22% YoY by volume, +20% by value), and the average ticket is shrinking (~₹1,217) as small-value use spreads. Person-to-merchant UPI carries zero MDR for most transactions, so the rail itself is a loss leader. Governance: RBI (licensing, data localisation) and NPCI (runs UPI and the switch).
- **Size & growth.** Metric: UPI transaction volume and value (network-operator data; P2P + P2M). Use volume for adoption and value for economics, and give the month or fiscal year. Value grew from ~₹0.07 lakh crore in FY17 to ~₹314 lakh crore in FY26. Throughput check: 24.5B ÷ (31 days × 86,400 s) ≈ 9,150 transactions per second on average, and systems are sized for peaks several times higher. The ~49% is a share of real-time payments only; do not describe it as a share of "all global digital transactions".
- **Leaders.** UPI apps (May 2026 volume share): PhonePe ~46%, Google Pay ~33%, Paytm ~8%, so the top two hold ~79%; NPCI's 30% per-app cap has a compliance deadline of 31 Dec 2026. Card networks: Visa, Mastercard, RuPay. Payment aggregators and merchant acquirers: Razorpay, PayU, Pine Labs.
- **Value chain (four layers).** Governance and rails (RBI licensing and data localisation; NPCI runs UPI, clearing and settlement) → processing (issuer, acquirer and sponsor banks; switching at 99.9%+ uptime) → aggregation (payment aggregators and gateways: KYC, merchant onboarding, settlement) → interface (third-party apps, QR codes and soundboxes, UPI Lite, credit lines on UPI). Monetisation sits on top: lending, merchant software and devices, embedded insurance and wealth.
- **KPIs.** Transaction volume and TPV, success rate (technical declines), take rate (net revenue ÷ TPV), revenue per user and per merchant, fraud loss (bps of TPV), false-positive rate, uptime, merchant activation and retention, credit attach rate.
- **Formulas.**
  - Take rate = net payments revenue ÷ TPV; average ticket = value ÷ volume; fraud bps = fraud losses ÷ TPV × 10,000.
  - Merchant monetisation: a merchant taking ₹5 lakh a month on UPI pays ₹0 in MDR. A soundbox rented at ₹100 a month earns ₹1,200 a year; a ₹2 lakh working-capital loan at a 3% net spread earns ₹6,000 a year, **5× the device** [ILLUSTRATIVE]. Credit is where the money is.
- **Five forces.** Entry L-M (the interface layer is cheap to build, but licences, sponsor-bank tie-ups, fraud controls and trust take scale). Suppliers M-H (sponsor banks, NPCI rules and volume caps, and card networks set the economics). Buyers H (zero switching cost for consumers; merchants display several QR codes). Substitutes L (cash in rural and informal pockets; cards for credit and rewards).
- **Cost vs revenue drivers.** Cost: uptime and infrastructure (an "uptime tax"), fraud losses and risk operations, compliance (KYC, data localisation, licensing), acquisition (cashback, CAC), sponsor-bank fees. Revenue: credit (merchant and consumer loans, credit on UPI, cards), merchant value-added services (soundboxes, POS, software), MDR on cards and gateway payments, payout fees, embedded insurance and wealth distribution.
- **Key terms.** UPI, NPCI, MDR, payment aggregator/gateway (PA/PG), PPI (wallets), TPV, UPI Lite, credit line on UPI, RuPay credit card on UPI, account aggregator, SoftPOS, tokenisation, e-rupee (retail CBDC), 30% app-share cap.
- **Case tension.** A zero-MDR, zero-switching-cost rail is a **loss-leading acquisition funnel**: profit comes from credit, merchant VAS and embedded finance, while uptime and fraud control are the cost moats. The 30% cap may reshape concentration. Trap: applying Western interchange and take-rate economics to UPI volumes. For India payments regulation and zero-MDR monetisation, see `references/financial-services.md`.

## Pharmaceuticals
- **Snapshot.** Generics-led and export-heavy. India is the 3rd-largest producer of drugs by volume and supplies ~20% of global generic exports by volume, but ranks far lower by value [verify the exact rank]. The domestic market is **branded generics**: identical molecules sold under competing brands, promoted to doctors by medical representatives (MRs) and moved through a stockist chain. Chronic therapies lead growth (FY26: cardiac +13.6%, anti-diabetic +11.3%). Domestic margins are set by price control and GMP compliance; export margins by US generic price erosion, US FDA compliance and tariff risk. Regulators: CDSCO/DCGI (approvals, Schedule M GMP), NPPA (price control), and the US FDA for exports.
- **Size & growth.** Three measures. (a) Domestic market (retail/secondary sales): ₹2.46 lakh crore (~$28B) in FY26, +8.8% YoY. (b) Exports: $31.1B in FY26, only +2.1% in USD (after +9.4% in FY25); formulations and biologicals ~74%; the US takes ~30% (~$9.5B). (c) Total industry, domestic plus exports: ~$60B. Growth is price- and launch-led: in the year to April 2026, value grew ~10.3% while volume grew only ~0.8%. The industry ambition is ~$130B by 2030 [verify]. **Error fixed:** quoting ~16.6% CAGR for ~$60B (2026) → ~$130B (2030) → **~21%** a year (there are four compounding years; 16.6% is the five-year rate). Against ~9% domestic and ~2% export growth, the ambition needs a step change.
- **Leaders.** Sun Pharma (largest by revenue), Cipla, Dr Reddy's, Lupin, Zydus, Mankind, Alkem, and MNCs such as Abbott India.
- **Value chain.** R&D and regulatory (formulation development, bioequivalence studies, ANDA and domestic filings) → API and KSM sourcing (key starting materials and intermediates are heavily imported from one neighbouring country; PLI for bulk drugs) → manufacturing (API to finished dosage under GMP; batch release) → distribution (C&F agents → super-stockists and stockists → retail chemists, with MRs detailing doctors) → patient access (chemists, hospital pharmacies, e-pharmacy, government generic outlets, insurance and schemes).
- **KPIs.** Domestic growth split into price, volume and new launches; MR productivity (sales per rep per month); prescription share; chronic share of portfolio; US ANDA pipeline and approvals; US price erosion; regulatory observations (inspection findings, warning letters); batch failure rate; capacity utilisation; R&D as % of sales.
- **Formulas.**
  - Value growth ≈ volume growth + price growth + new-launch contribution (e.g. 10.3% ≈ 0.8% volume + ~9.5 points of price and new launches).
  - MR productivity = domestic sales ÷ number of reps ÷ 12 (e.g. ₹6,000 crore ÷ 12,000 reps ÷ 12 ≈ ₹4.2 lakh per rep per month [ILLUSTRATIVE]).
- **Five forces.** Entry L-M (thousands of small makers exist, but doctor-facing brand equity, field-force scale and GMP/US-FDA compliance gate the profitable tiers). Suppliers M-H (KSM and intermediate imports are concentrated in one country). Buyers M (individual patients have little power, but price caps, hospital and government tenders and insurers do; in exports, consolidated US wholesale buyers are H). Substitutes M. **Error fixed:** rating substitutes High "because of generics and biosimilars" → M. The domestic market is already generic, so the live threat is a competing brand of the same molecule, plus trade generics and government generic outlets.
- **Cost vs revenue drivers.** Cost: API and KSM prices (import-dependent), quality compliance (US FDA, revised Schedule M), the MR field force, R&D and patent litigation, cold chain, track-and-trace. Revenue: chronic-therapy growth, new launches, annual price revisions (WPI-linked for price-controlled drugs; capped at ~10% a year for the rest), US generic launches as patents expire, complex generics and biosimilars, CDMO contracts, PLI.
- **Key terms.** API, KSM, formulation, ANDA, Para IV challenge, bioequivalence, NLEM/DPCO (price control), NPPA, Schedule M (GMP), branded vs trade generics, detailing, stockist/super-stockist, CDMO, biosimilar, patent cliff.
- **Case tension.** Domestic margin turns on price caps, GMP compliance and MR productivity, not patents; export margin turns on US price erosion and FDA compliance; growth comes from global patent cliffs and complex generics. Trap: importing US patent-cliff and drug-pricing-reform logic wholesale into the domestic Indian market (for US economics, see `references/healthcare-life-sciences.md`).

## Quick commerce
- **Snapshot.** 10–30-minute delivery of groceries and daily needs from dark stores, now spreading into electronics, beauty and gifting. GMV was ~$13–14B in FY26, growing ~120% YoY, which is ~17% of India's ~$80B online-retail GMV. It is a three-player oligopoly moving from growth at all costs to store-level contribution breakeven; the leader turned adjusted-EBITDA-positive in Q1 FY27 (~0.6% of order value). Oversight: DPIIT (FDI rules shape inventory-led vs marketplace models), FSSAI, municipal zoning for dark stores.
- **Size & growth.** Metric: gross order value (GMV) of 10–30-minute platforms (research estimate). ~$13–14B in FY26 (~+120%, so FY25 was ~$6B); the early-2026 run-rate was ~₹11,000 crore a month (~$15B a year). 2030 forecasts range from ~$40B to ~$70B, so quote a range. **Definitions do not mix:** GMV/GOV (gross, before discounts) vs NOV (net of discounts) vs revenue. When a player shifts to an inventory-led model, reported revenue jumps because it now books the full sale: the leader's revenue rose ~6.5× YoY in Q1 FY27 while its order value rose ~1.9×. A ~$4B "market size" seen in some reports is narrow platform revenue, not GMV.
- **Leaders.** Blinkit (Eternal) ~44–50% of GMV, Zepto ~20–30%, Swiggy Instamart ~20–25% (analyst estimates); followers Flipkart Minutes, BigBasket (Tata), JioMart, Amazon Now. Scale marker: the leader ran ~900M orders in FY26 from 2,243 dark stores at year-end, i.e. **~1,100 orders per store per day** even on the year-end store count (more on the average count).
- **Value chain.** Sourcing (direct brand partnerships, private label, micro-market assortment, working capital) → dark-store operations (~2–3 km radius; picking, packing, replenishment) → technology (SKU-level forecasting, rider routing, dynamic pricing, basket recommendations) → last mile (gig riders, batching, delivery-time incentives) → monetisation (product margin or commission, delivery/handling/platform fees, brand advertising, private label, subscriptions).
- **KPIs.** Orders per store per day, AOV, contribution per order, delivery cost per order, picker productivity (orders per picker-hour), fill rate and stock-outs, delivery time, dark-store density, cohort order frequency, ad income as % of GMV, months to store breakeven.
- **Formulas.**
  - Contribution per order = AOV × (product margin + ad income %) + customer fees − delivery − picking and packing − wastage and payment cost.
  - Store breakeven orders per day = fixed store cost per day ÷ contribution per order.
  - Example [ILLUSTRATIVE]: AOV ₹500 × 18% = ₹90, + ₹20 fees; − ₹60 delivery − ₹15 picking and packing − ₹10 wastage and payment = **₹25 per order**. A fixed store cost of ₹30,000 a day (rent, staff, power) ÷ ₹25 = **1,200 orders a day to break even**, close to the leader's ~1,100 and consistent with its near-zero margin. At an AOV of ₹350 the same stack gives ₹63 + ₹20 − ₹85 = **−₹2 per order**, and no density breaks even, so AOV is the first lever. Delivery at ₹60–90 is 11–30% of a ₹300–550 basket.
- **Five forces.** Entry L-M (dark-store density, capital to fund losses and brand-partner terms deter entrants, yet well-funded e-commerce and retail groups keep entering). Suppliers M (FMCG brands need the channel for impulse buys and launches but resist margin squeeze; riders). Buyers H (multi-app, zero switching cost, fee-sensitive). Substitutes M-H (kirana home delivery, supermarkets, scheduled e-grocery).
- **Cost vs revenue drivers.** Cost: last-mile rider cost, dark-store rent and staff, discounts and CAC, fresh wastage, technology. Revenue: product margin (inventory-led) or commission (marketplace), delivery/handling/platform fees, brand advertising, private label, category expansion that lifts AOV.
- **Key terms.** Dark store, GMV vs NOV vs revenue, inventory-led vs marketplace model, orders per store per day, AOV, fill rate, picker productivity, cohort frequency, store-level contribution, micro-market assortment.
- **Case tension.** Profitability is a **density game**: orders per store per day and AOV spread fixed store and rider cost, and ads and private label fund the gap, so analyse store-level contribution after delivery cost. Trap: judging the model on platform GMV growth, or comparing players' GMV, NOV and revenue as if they were the same measure. For the incumbent's side, see Drills B and O in `references/india-guesstimates-and-cases.md`.

## Semiconductors & electronics
- **Snapshot.** A large consumer and a negligible producer. India consumes ~$52–60B of chips a year (2024–25), almost all imported, and has no front-end fab in commercial production yet (first silicon is targeted for late 2026). Its strength is design: global chip companies run large design centres in India. Policy runs through the India Semiconductor Mission (ISM): 12 approved projects (~₹1.64 lakh crore of cumulative investment), made up of 1 silicon fab, 2 compound-semiconductor fabs and 9 assembly-and-test (ATMP/OSAT) units, the first of which began operating in early 2026. ISM 1.0 offered ~₹76,000 crore (up to 50% fiscal support, pari passu); ISM 2.0 was approved with ~₹1,27,500 crore. Gujarat is the main cluster. Ministry: MeitY.
- **Size & growth.** Metric: semiconductor **consumption** (the value of chips used in India across mobile, IT, auto and industrial), not domestic production. ~$52–54B (2024–25, reference estimate; other vendors say $45–60B), projected ~$103B by 2030. **Error fixed:** quoting "12.1% CAGR, 2025–30" for $52B → $103.4B → ~14.7% over five years; 12.1% is the six-year rate and fits only if $52B is a 2024 figure. State the base year before the rate.
- **Leaders.** Fab: Tata Electronics with PSMC (Dholera; ~₹91,000 crore; mature nodes; full completion ~2028). OSAT/ATMP: Micron (Sanand; the mission's first operating unit, Feb 2026), Kaynes Semicon (commercial production Mar 2026), CG Power with Renesas and Stars, Tata (Assam). Design: global fabless and IDM design centres, plus Indian fabless start-ups under the design-linked incentive (DLI) scheme.
- **Value chain.** Chip design and IP (specification, RTL, verification, tape-out; India's strength) → wafer fabrication (front end: lithography, etch, deposition; the capital-heavy gap) → ATMP/OSAT (back end: assembly, test, packaging; the first localisation win) → electronics manufacturing (EMS assembly of phones, IT hardware, auto electronics) → end markets (mobile, auto, industrial, telecom, defence, data centres).
- **KPIs.** Import dependence (share of chip demand imported), wafer starts per month (WSPM), fab utilisation and yield, OSAT units per day and test yield, approved vs commissioned capex, subsidy disbursed vs committed, domestic value addition in electronics, design tape-outs.
- **Formulas.**
  - Gross dies per wafer ≈ π × (d/2)² ÷ S − π × d ÷ √(2S), for wafer diameter d and die area S. Cost per good die = wafer cost ÷ (gross dies × yield).
  - Example [ILLUSTRATIVE]: a 300 mm wafer and a 100 mm² die give 70,686 ÷ 100 − 942.5 ÷ 14.1 ≈ 707 − 67 ≈ 640 gross dies. At 80% yield that is 512 good dies, so a $5,000 wafer costs ~$9.8 per good die; at 60% yield, ~$13.0. **Yield is the fab P&L.**
  - Domestic value addition = (output value − imported inputs) ÷ output value.
- **Five forces.** Entry L (multi-billion-dollar fab capex, process know-how and customer qualification; a ~50% subsidy lowers the barrier but does not remove it). Suppliers **H** (concentrated equipment, materials, EDA and process-licence vendors). Buyers M-H (global OEMs and EMS firms multi-source on price and qualification). Substitutes L-M (imports are the real alternative to local supply; nothing substitutes for chips themselves).
- **Cost vs revenue drivers.** Cost: fab capex and depreciation, imported equipment and materials, ultra-pure water and stable power, talent, yield-ramp losses. Revenue: domestic electronics demand (mobile, auto, industrial), trusted-source demand from government and defence, export OSAT contracts, AI and data-centre demand, design services.
- **Key terms.** Fab, foundry, fabless, IDM, OSAT/ATMP, node (nm), mature vs leading-edge node, WSPM, yield, tape-out, EDA, compound semiconductor (SiC, GaN), DLI scheme, ISM.
- **Case tension.** Design-strong, fab-weak: policy and capital go to back-end OSAT first (faster, less capital-intensive) while the first fab ramps on mature nodes for auto, industrial and power uses, not the leading edge. Trap: treating the ~$52–60B "market" as domestic production when almost all of it is imported. For global semiconductor economics, see `references/technology-media-telecommunications.md`.

## Sizing traps across sectors

Most wrong sector numbers come from four slips. Run these checks before any figure reaches a slide or an answer. The drill versions are in `references/case-cracking-drills.md`: Part D (Error Hunt), especially D1 (CAGR windows), D12 (unit mismatches) and D15 (airport footfall), plus E5 (CAGR and the rule of 72).

### 1. One sector, three sizes: define the metric first
Every sector has several legitimate "sizes". They measure different things, so setting one against another produces fake gaps and fake growth. State the metric, scope, as-of date and currency in the same breath as the number.

| Sector | Size A | Size B | Size C | Why they differ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Banking | Deposits ~₹262 lakh cr (Mar-2026) | Credit ~₹214 lakh cr (Mar-2026) | PSB + private-bank assets ~₹287 lakh cr (FY25) | Liability side vs asset side; assets also include investments and reserves; different dates |
| E-commerce | e-retail GMV ~$65–66B (CY2025) | Online retail ~$75–80B (other trackers, FY basis) | "$100B+ e-commerce" | Year basis and scope: physical goods only vs adding travel, food delivery, digital services, B2B |
| Insurance | Gross premium ₹11.93 lakh cr (FY25) | Claims paid ₹8.36 lakh cr (FY25) | AUM ₹74.4 lakh cr (Mar-2025) | Annual inflow vs annual outflow vs accumulated stock |
| Food & beverage | GVA ~₹2.24 lakh cr (~$27B, FY24) | Gross processed-food market ~$355B (2024) | Packaged FMCG ~$110B | Value added vs output value vs a branded-goods subset |
| Pharmaceuticals | Domestic market ~$28B (FY26) | Exports ~$31B (FY26) | Total industry ~$60B | Domestic sales vs exports vs their sum |

Rule: when two figures for the "same" market differ by more than ~1.5×, assume a definition gap, not a data error, and find the definition before choosing one.

### 2. CAGR sanity: count the compounding years
- Compounding years = end year − start year. 2026 → 2031 is five years, not six, and the label must match the endpoints.
- Rule of 72: doubling time ≈ 72 ÷ rate (%). A market that doubles in four years grows ~18% a year (exactly 18.9%); one growing at 12% doubles in ~6 years.
- Reverse check: end = start × (1 + r)ⁿ. If the quoted rate does not return the quoted end value, the rate, the endpoints or the window is wrong.
- An average of yearly growth rates is not a CAGR. The average overstates when growth is volatile: food-processing GVA grew ~6.6% a year on average but ~5.9% compounded over FY15–FY24.

**Worked correction 1: right rate, wrong label [ILLUSTRATIVE forecast].** An aviation forecast reads "$16.5B (2026) → $29.0B (2031), CAGR 11.9%, 2024–30". The ratio is 29.0 ÷ 16.5 = 1.76, and over five years 1.76^(1/5) − 1 = **11.9%**. The rate is right for 2026–31. Over the six years the label implies, 11.9% would turn $16.5B into ~$32.4B, about 12% too high. **Error fixed:** "11.9% CAGR, 2024–30" → "11.9% CAGR, 2026–31" (the rate fits the endpoints only over five compounding years, and anyone projecting from a 2024 base would overshoot).

**Worked correction 2: calendar years counted instead of gaps [ILLUSTRATIVE forecast].** A defence-market forecast reads "$31.8B (2026) → $38.7B (2030), CAGR 4.05%". The ratio is 1.217 over four compounding years, so 1.217^(1/4) − 1 = **~5.0%**. At 4.05%, 2030 would be ~$37.2B, not $38.7B. **Error fixed:** "4.05%" → "~5.0%" (4.05% is close to the five-year rate of 4.0%; 2026–30 has four compounding periods).

### 3. Throughput counts are not people: airport footfall
- Airport footfall (~412M in FY25) counts arrivals plus departures at every airport, domestic and international, so each domestic one-way flight adds **two** to the counter. Domestic airline passengers, counted once per flight segment, were ~165M in FY25 and 167.7M in FY26.
- Reconcile: 2 × ~165M domestic segments ≈ ~330M domestic footfall, which leaves ~80M of international footfall inside the ~412M [derived].
- Then convert segments → trips → people: 167.7M segments ÷ ~2.1 segments per round trip ÷ ~2.5 round trips per flyer a year ≈ **~32M unique domestic flyers** [ILLUSTRATIVE], a small fraction of the footfall headline.
- **Error fixed:** "~412M people fly in India each year" → ~412M is airport throughput; domestic journeys are ~168M segments, and unique flyers are roughly 30–40M (count what one event adds to the counter, then remove repeat trips).
- The same trap appears in toll crossings vs unique vehicles, metro entries vs unique riders, port TEUs including transhipment, app downloads vs users, and SIM connections vs subscribers (tele-density above 100%).

### 4. Unit slips: ₹ vs $, lakh vs crore
- Ladder: 1 lakh = 10⁵; 1 crore = 100 lakh = 10⁷ (10 million); 1 lakh crore = 10¹² (₹1 trillion); ₹1 billion = 100 crore. At ~₹88/$ (FY26), ₹1 lakh crore ≈ $11.4B, ₹1,000 crore ≈ $114M and ₹1 billion ≈ $11.4M.
- ₹ read as $: a chart of listed hospital chains labelled "$1,079B" means ₹1,079B ≈ $12B. A "trillion-dollar" Indian mid-cap is always a unit slip.
- Mixed currencies in one row: "$4.8B, ₹1.0B, $0.11B" puts ~$11M next to billions. Convert everything to one currency before ranking.
- Crore vs million: 2.9 crore = 29 million, not 290 million.
- Scope inside a unit count: a passenger-vehicle count is not the vehicle market (~4.6M PVs vs ~28.3M vehicles in FY26), and a vehicle "market" in lakh units is not a rupee market.
- Fiscal vs calendar year: FY26 = April 2025 to March 2026. Mixing an FY base with a CY end shifts the window by a quarter and distorts growth.
- Exchange-rate drift: a rupee series converted at different rates each year shows fake dollar growth or decline. Grow the series in rupees and convert once, at a stated rate.

**Default habit.** Give the metric, scope, date and unit with every number, then test it against one independent anchor: a per-capita figure, a bottom-up count, or the largest company you know.
