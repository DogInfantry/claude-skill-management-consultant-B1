# Case-Type Cheat Sheets — One-Page Cards

These cards are for the 90 seconds between hearing a case prompt and drawing a structure. Each card compresses one case type into a fixed pre-flight checklist: the words to capture, the objective question, the clarifiers worth asking, the one-line equation, the buckets, the numbers that usually decide the case, and the shape of the close. Coaches can use the same cards to check a candidate's opening and close against a standard.

A card is a checklist, not a template. Tailor every label to the client's own words ("revenue from bottled-water sales", not "revenue"), and drop any bucket the facts have already ruled out.

**How the cards relate to the rest of the skill**
- `case-types.md` holds the full playbook per case type: the detailed issue tree, key questions, and what a great answer looks like. Go there once a branch needs depth.
- `case-interview.md` holds the 7-step case process, the opening scripts per case type, and the scoring rubric. The cards plug into its Steps 1–3 and Step 7.
- `case-cracking-drills.md` holds the 90-second scaffolds and the drills to rehearse them. Use the cards to self-check a cold structure after a drill.
- `case-pattern-library.md` holds the archetype patterns and their solution logic (the "aha"). Once a card has located the problem, match it to a pattern there.
- `case-practice-index.md` indexes every case and drill by sector, case type and difficulty, and holds the four-week ladder these cards are read against. Go there to choose what to run next.

**The routine, for every card**
1. Write down the keywords and restate the problem in one sentence.
2. Ask the objective first.
3. Ask 1–2 clarifiers by default. Go up to 4 only if each answer would change your structure.
4. Write the equation spine, then hang the numbered buckets off it. Tailor the labels.
5. State a hypothesis and the branch you will test first.
6. Close with the answer, 2–3 reasons, risks, and next steps placed on a realistic timeline (see [Recommendation guardrails](#recommendation-guardrails)).

## Card 1 — Profitability

**Keywords to capture & restate**
- Profit or margin? Declining in absolute terms, or "below the industry average"? The second is a relative gap and needs a peer comparison.
- Since when? One product or region, or all of them?
- If the prompt says "the cost structure is poor", note it, but still confirm whether revenue is in scope.

**Objective first**
"What does success look like: which margin or profit level, by when?" For example, back to the industry-average EBITDA margin within two years.

**Clarifier bank** (each answer changes the tree)
1. *Industry-wide or just us?* Industry-wide leads with the external branch (regulation, demand, input costs). Company-specific leads with our price, volume, mix and operations.
2. *Since when, and sudden or gradual?* A sudden drop points to an event (a sourcing change, a new entrant, a price cut). A gradual one points to structural drift.
3. *Which products, segments or geographies?* This decides whether you split the tree by product or by region first.
4. *Revenue, cost or both?* This decides whether you build one half of the tree or both.

**Equation spine**
`Profit = (P − VC) × Q − FC − one-offs`, with `Revenue = Σ (P × Q)` across the product mix.

**Approach buckets**
1. **Price.** Split each driver into internal and external causes.
   - Internal: list-price changes, discount leakage, cost pass-through (or the lack of it), transfer pricing, mix.
   - External: price wars, price caps or regulated trade margins, customer sentiment, the supply–demand balance.
2. **Volume.**
   - Internal: the 4A access lens (awareness, accessibility, affordability, availability) and capacity.
   - External: the economic cycle, regulation, competitor moves, industry trends, geography.
3. **Mix.** A shift toward lower-margin products or channels can cut profit even when price and volume both hold.
4. **Operating cost.** Split into fixed (depreciation, salaries, rent, insurance), variable (raw material, fuel, freight, commissions) and semi-variable (utilities, maintenance). If that split does not fit the business cleanly, walk the value chain instead: procurement → production → storage → transport → distribution → after-sales.
5. **One-off / non-operating cost.** Write-offs, impairments, restructuring, litigation, FX losses. Strip these out first so they do not distort the run-rate diagnosis.
6. **External overlay.** If the problem is industry-wide, run PESTLE to find the cause.

**Numbers that crack it**
- **Weight each line item by its share of the total.** A 30–35% rise in an input that is 25% of COGS lifts COGS by 25% × 30–35% = **7.5–8.75%**, not 30–35%.
- **Capacity utilisation.** A line that could fill 500 bottles a shift fills 300, so utilisation is 60%. With ₹15,000 of fixed cost per shift [ILLUSTRATIVE], fixed cost per bottle is ₹50 at 300 but ₹30 at 500. Filling the line cuts it by **40%**.
- **Potential capacity.** Three machines run where five fit, so the site's output ceiling could rise by 5/3 − 1 = **67%**. That is a lever only if the demand exists.
- **Contribution to the change.** Rank segments by how much each one contributed to the fall, not by its share of revenue (see `case-interview.md`, Additional Case-Craft Moves).

**Customer-access check (4A)**
Use it when volume falls while the market grows. Ask which A broke: awareness (the advertising budget was cut), accessibility (a key channel was lost), affordability (the entry pack was withdrawn) or availability (stock-outs, distributors dropped).

**Risks to name**
- Cutting a cost that drives revenue, such as sales coverage or product quality.
- Treating a one-off as run-rate, or the reverse.
- A mix shift hiding behind a stable average price.
- A fix that competitors can match within a quarter.

**The close**
"Profit fell because of X, which explains Y of the Z pp decline. Fix it with A now, B over the next 6–12 months and C structurally. The main risk is R. The next step is N."

**Coach tips**
- Even when the prompt blames the cost structure, spend five seconds asking whether revenue is in scope. It prevents a half-built tree.
- Brainstorm this business's real revenue streams rather than writing a generic "price × volume". A museum, for example, earns from tickets, events, food and beverage, merchandise, and memberships or sponsorship.
- State the whole value chain aloud, then ask where the problem sits. One targeted question replaces a fishing expedition.
- Ask both capacity questions: are we using the capacity we have, and could we install more? The first is a fixed-cost absorption problem. The second matters only if demand exists.
- "Standard costs" are a budgeting method, not a one-off cost. Reserve the one-off bucket for write-offs, impairments, restructuring and similar non-recurring items.
- If the client lags the industry average but the shock is industry-wide (input inflation, a tax change), the shock cannot be the explanation, because peers face it too. The gap lies in what peers do differently: pass-through clauses, hedging, leaner designs.
- **Error fixed:** "copper is up 30–35%, so costs are up 30–35%" → costs rise by copper's share × its price change: 25% × 30–35% = 7.5–8.75% of COGS (a line-item percentage must be weighted by that line's share of the total).
- Structure the close by time frame: quick wins, then process fixes, then strategic shifts. Place each item on a realistic timeline.

## Card 2 — Market Entry

**Keywords to capture & restate**
- Which market: a geography, a segment or a category?
- Is the question *whether* to enter, *how* to enter, or *which* of several markets?
- The target (share, revenue or a break-even year) and the timeline.

**Objective first**
"Why enter: profit, share and scale, or a strategic reason such as a capability, following a key customer, or pre-empting a rival?" The objective sets the hurdle. A profit objective needs a payback inside the planning horizon. A strategic objective can accept a longer loss period, but you should name how long.

**Clarifier bank** (each answer changes the tree)
1. *Company capability.* What do we bring: brand, capital, technology, distribution? This sets the weight on "can we win" and pushes toward a build or a buy.
2. *Product.* Are we entering with a full line, a niche or a single product? This changes the sizing base and the go-to-market plan.
3. *Target segment.* Urban or rural, premium or mass, B2B or B2C? This changes the sizing and the channel.
4. *Competitive structure.* A dominant incumbent or a fragmented field? This changes the share assumption and the entry mode.

**Equation spine**
`Entry = Should we (attractive + economics clear the hurdle) + Can we win (right to win) + How (mode + go-to-market)`

Entry economics: `Profit = Size (units) × Share × (P − VC) − FC`, and `Break-even share = FC ÷ [Size (units) × (P − VC)]`.

**Approach buckets**
1. **Should we enter?**
   - Market: size, growth, customer need, profit pool.
   - Competition: structure, rivals' USPs, likely response.
   - Regulation and red tape: licensing, IP safety, ownership or local-content rules, direction of reform.
   - Operational feasibility: whether raw material, labour and transport are available locally.
   - Economics: investment, unit economics, break-even share, payback.
2. **Can we win?**
   - Financial strength, brand and positioning, operational capability, technology.
   - Evidence from the company's past expansions.
3. **How to enter?**
   - Mode: greenfield; brownfield (buy and upgrade an existing asset); acquisition; JV or partnership; licensing or franchising.
   - Go-to-market: positioning, pricing, promotion.
   - Channel: direct or distributor, online or offline.
   - Sequencing: pilot, then scale, then expand.

**Numbers that crack it** [ILLUSTRATIVE]
- The market is 20 lakh units a year, unit contribution (P − VC) is ₹2,000, and entry fixed cost is ₹60 crore a year. Break-even share = ₹60 crore ÷ (20 lakh × ₹2,000 = ₹400 crore) = **15%**.
- If a new entrant can realistically reach 5–8% share in three years, standalone entry fails. That pushes the answer toward acquiring share or partnering.
- Payback = upfront investment ÷ annual cash contribution. Compare it with the objective's hurdle.

**Customer-access check (4A)**
This belongs in the "how" bucket.
- Awareness: a new brand starts at zero.
- Accessibility: channel reach and ease of purchase.
- Affordability: price point and pack size.
- Availability: shelf presence, including rural distribution depth.

**Risks to name**
- Incumbent retaliation (price cuts, trade-margin wars).
- Regulatory change.
- Localisation and execution failure.
- Currency exposure.
- An overestimated share assumption.

**The close**
1. Should we enter? Yes, no, or "yes, via X".
2. Why? Market attractiveness plus capability.
3. How? Entry mode plus go-to-market.
4. Risks and mitigations.

Time frame: pilot, then scale, then expand. Name a kill criterion, e.g. share below N% by month 18.

**Coach tips**
- Ask the objective before sizing the market. Sizing first wastes minutes on a number whose hurdle you do not yet know.
- **Error fixed:** "Profit = Market size (₹) × Share × (Price − Cost) − Fixed" → with size in ₹, use Size (₹) × Share × contribution margin % − FC. For example, at a ₹10,000 price the 20 lakh-unit market is worth ₹2,000 crore and the contribution margin is 20%, so ₹2,000 crore × 15% × 20% = ₹60 crore, which equals FC (multiplying a rupee market by a rupee margin gives rupees squared, a meaningless unit). The (P − VC) form works only when size is in units and VC is variable cost per unit.
- "Financial feasibility" without a break-even share or payback figure is an opinion. Always produce one of the two.
- Run the value-chain check: can the local chain (procurement → production → distribution → sales → service) support us? A missing link pushes the mode toward partnership.
- Barriers to exit (specialised assets, labour rules, long leases) are economic as well as regulatory. Ask about them before committing capital.
- Keep the entry motive visible throughout the case, and use the company's past expansions as evidence of capability.

## Card 3 — New Product Launch

**Keywords to capture & restate**
- The product, the target market and the timeline.
- Is the question *whether* to launch or *how* to launch?
- The price point, if one is given.

**Objective first**
"What must the launch achieve, and by when?" Options include a revenue or profit target, share in a new segment, defending the core against a rival, or rounding out the portfolio.

**Clarifier bank** (each answer changes the tree)
1. *What exactly is the product?* Its features, SKUs and pack sizes, and whether it is new to the world or new only to the company. This decides whether demand is sized from analogues or from an existing category.
2. *Who is the target customer?* Geography, income band, demography. This sets how demand is segmented.
3. *Is the go/no-go already decided?* If not, run the entry gate first.
4. *Which existing assets can we use?* Channels, brand, capacity, and the risk of cannibalising current products. This shapes the operations plan and the incremental economics.

**Equation spine**
`Launch = Gate (should we? — Card 2) → Plan (price + make + move + market) → Monitor (KPIs + kill criteria)`

Incremental profit = new-product contribution − cannibalised contribution − incremental FC.

**Approach buckets**
1. **Gate.** Run the market-entry test from Card 2: demand, competition, right to win, economics.
2. **Product and audience.** Features, SKUs and packaging. Segment by geography, income and demography.
3. **Pricing.** Elasticity, positioning, USP, and the floor and ceiling from Card 4.
4. **Financial plan.** Unit cost, contribution, launch investment, break-even.
5. **Operational plan.**
   - Inbound: make in-house, contract-manufacture, or import.
   - Inventory policy.
   - Outbound: channels and the margin each one takes.
6. **Marketing and communication.** Strategy, advertising, consumer promotion and trade push, service and after-sales.
7. **Launch KPIs and kill criteria.** Trial rate, repeat rate, distribution reach, and contribution per unit by month N.

**Numbers that crack it** [ILLUSTRATIVE]
- **Net out cannibalisation.** A new variant sells 10 lakh units at ₹30 contribution, which gives ₹3 crore. But 40% of its buyers switch from an existing SKU that earns ₹40 contribution, costing 4 lakh × ₹40 = ₹1.6 crore. The incremental gain is **₹1.4 crore** before incremental fixed cost, less than half the headline figure.
- **Launch volume = min(demand, capacity).** A launch plan that ignores the supply ramp overstates year-1 sales.

**Customer-access check (4A)**
This is central to any launch.
- Awareness is zero on day one.
- Availability depends on distribution being in place before the advertising runs.
- Affordability often rests on an entry-size pack.
- Accessibility covers where and how the customer can buy.

**Risks to name**
- Cannibalisation.
- Demand overestimation.
- Channel resistance, especially over trade margins.
- A slow supply ramp-up.
- Fast imitation by rivals.

**The close**
1. Launch or not.
2. Target segment and price.
3. Channel and operating model.
4. KPIs and kill criteria.
5. Risks.

Time frame: pilot market, then regional roll-out, then national.

**Coach tips**
- A launch is market entry plus pricing plus operations. Run the go/no-go first and only then build the plan. Say the overlap with Card 2 aloud so the interviewer can see you are not counting anything twice.
- Describe the product by its features, SKUs and packaging. Pack size is often the affordability lever.
- Name the kill criteria before the launch, not after it.
- A launch with no KPIs cannot be managed. Name two leading indicators (trial, repeat) and one lagging one (contribution).

## Card 4 — Pricing

**Keywords to capture & restate**
- Pricing a new product, or re-pricing an existing one?
- One price, or a range or architecture?
- B2B or B2C? Any stated constraints?

**Objective first**
"Profit maximisation, penetration for share, premium positioning, or competitive parity?" The objective decides where between the floor and the ceiling the price lands.

**Clarifier bank** (each answer changes the tree)
1. *Company positioning.* Premium, mass or low-cost?
2. *Product value.* The USP and how it differs from the customer's next-best alternative.
3. *Customer.* Who buys, and how price-sensitive are they?
4. *Competition.* What do rivals and substitutes charge?

Optional constraints: a cost floor, regulation (price caps), capacity.

**Equation spine**
`Floor (VC short run; full cost long run) ≤ Price ≤ Ceiling (customer value / WTP)`, positioned against the competition (low, parity or premium). Then `Profit = (P − VC) × Q(P) − FC`.

**Approach buckets**
1. **Cost floor.**
   - Short-run floor: variable cost per unit. Contribution (P − VC) must be positive.
   - Long-run floor: full cost, including allocated fixed cost.
   - Sunk R&D is not part of the floor. Recovering it is a target, not a constraint.
2. **Value ceiling.** Value perception (economic value to the customer), price sensitivity, segmentation (premium vs mass, B2B vs B2C).
3. **Competitive reference.** Rival price points and substitutes, adjusted for our differentiation premium or discount.
4. **Decision.** Choose one of each:
   - an objective: penetration, premium or parity;
   - a method: cost-plus, value-based or competitor-based;
   - tactics: bundling, discounts, dynamic pricing, subscription.
5. **Checks.**
   - Volume response (elasticity).
   - Whether capacity can serve the volume the price creates.
   - Likely competitor response.

**Numbers that crack it** [ILLUSTRATIVE]
- Price is ₹100 and variable cost is ₹60, so contribution is ₹40.
  - **A 10% cut** to ₹90 leaves ₹30 of contribution. Volume must rise by 40/30 − 1 = **33%** just to hold total contribution. If the plant has only 20% headroom, the cut cannot pay back even if demand responds.
  - **A 10% rise** to ₹110 lifts contribution to ₹50. The business can lose up to 1 − 40/50 = **20%** of volume and still break even.
- Rule: required volume change = old contribution per unit ÷ new contribution per unit − 1.

**Customer-access check (4A)**
- Affordability: the pack size, instalment or subscription option that brings the price within reach.
- Awareness: whether customers know the value the price is based on.
- Availability: whether the product is stocked at that price point.
- Accessibility: whether the purchase is easy to complete.

Keep affordability separate from competitiveness. The first is about the customer's ability to pay; the second belongs in the competition bucket.

**Risks to name**
- Demand estimate wrong.
- Price war.
- Brand dilution from discounting.
- Customer resistance or churn.
- Regulatory scrutiny of the price.

**The close**
1. What price? Give a number or a tight range.
2. Why? Floor, ceiling and competitive position.
3. Impact on volume, revenue and profit.
4. Risks and mitigations.

Time frame: pilot or test the price, then optimise, then build pricing power.

**Coach tips**
- **Error fixed:** "Profit = (Price − Cost) × Volume" → "Profit = (Price − Variable cost) × Volume − Fixed cost" (the fixed cost listed in the floor must be subtracted once, outside the bracket; a full unit cost inside the bracket is only true at one volume and breaks every price–volume trade-off).
- "Pricing = Cost + Customer value + Competition" is a triangulation, not a sum. Never add the three together.
- The floor and ceiling are *prices*: the minimum price comes from cost, the maximum from value.
- Competitor-based pricing starts with the gap between our offer and the rival's, then sets a premium or a discount. "Price ≈ competitor price" ignores differentiation.
- Check supply against the price: a lower price creates volume, so confirm the plant and channel can deliver it.
- The usual strategy list mixes objectives (penetration), methods (cost-plus, value-based) and tactics (dynamic pricing). Choose one of each rather than treating them as alternatives.
- Never state a price without its volume and profit impact.
- Pricing independently in response to a rival is legitimate. Agreeing prices with a rival is not (see [Recommendation guardrails](#recommendation-guardrails)).

## Card 5 — M&A / Investment

**Keywords to capture & restate**
- Is the buyer strategic (a company) or financial (a fund)?
- Stake: full or partial, control or minority?
- Is the question "should we", "how much", or "which target"? What is the timeline?

**Objective first**
"What is the deal for: market entry, a capability, share, profit, or a financial return?" Strategic buyers value synergies. Financial buyers value returns and the exit.

**Clarifier bank** (each answer changes the tree)
1. *Acquirer.* Why M&A, and why now: a growth gap or a capability gap? The answer sets the rationale test and the build/partner alternatives.
2. *Target.* What does it do, what is its market position, and what are its strengths? This sets the diligence focus.
3. *Deal scope.* Full or partial, control or minority? A minority stake cannot deliver most synergies and makes integration largely irrelevant.
4. *Success criteria.* Synergies, IRR or payback? This decides the valuation lens.

Optional: timeline, regulatory approval, integration constraints.

**Equation spine**
`M&A = Should we (fit) + Can we create value (synergies) + Are we paying right (price)`

The value test:

`Value created = PV(net synergies) − Premium − Integration cost > 0`, where `Premium = Price − Standalone value`.

Equivalently, the walk-away price:

`Price < Standalone value + PV(net synergies) − Integration cost`

**Approach buckets**
1. **Strategic fit.** Long-term strategy, synergy types, competitive impact, and the alternatives (build or partner).
2. **Target diligence.**
   - Revenue quality: P × V, how sustainable growth is, customer concentration.
   - Costs and margins: the fixed vs variable split.
   - Balance sheet: debt, cash flow, working capital.
   - Operations, technology and talent.
   - Red flags: legal, regulatory, accounting.
3. **Synergies.**
   - Cost: procurement, operations, overhead.
   - Revenue: cross-sell, channels, pricing.
   - Dis-synergies: customer loss, channel conflict.
   - Reality check: time to capture, cost to achieve, probability.
4. **Valuation and deal.**
   - Standalone value: DCF, trading multiples, precedent deals.
   - Synergy PV and the walk-away price.
   - Structure: cash vs stock, earn-outs, full vs partial.
5. **Integration and risk.**
   - Integration plan, culture, key people, antitrust.
   - For a financial buyer: the exit route.

**Numbers that crack it** [ILLUSTRATIVE, ₹ crore]
- Assumptions: the target's standalone value is 1,000. Post-tax synergies are 60 a year, starting in year 3, capitalised at 10%. Integration costs 100.
- Synergy value at the end of year 2 is 60 / 10% = 600. Discounted two years, that is 600 / 1.1² ≈ **496, about 500**.
- **Walk-away price** ≈ 1,000 + 496 − 100 ≈ **1,400**.
- **Bid.** Paying 1,200 (a 20% premium) leaves the acquirer about **200** of value (1,000 + 496 − 100 − 1,200 = 196). Paying 1,400 hands all of it to the seller.

**Customer-access check (4A)**
Use it only to test revenue synergies. Does the combination improve availability (a wider distribution footprint), accessibility, or awareness (cross-selling to an installed base)? If not, the revenue synergies are probably overstated.

**Risks to name**
- Overpaying in a competitive auction.
- Synergy shortfall or delay.
- Integration and culture clash.
- Losing key talent.
- Antitrust or regulatory approval.
- Hidden liabilities.

**The close**
1. Do the deal, yes or no.
2. Why: fit plus synergies.
3. **Price guidance**: the walk-away price and a target bid.
4. Integration plan.
5. Risks and mitigations.

Time frame: diligence and planning, then integration and quick synergies, then full value realisation.

**Coach tips**
- **Error fixed:** "bid Price = Value + Synergies − Costs − Risk" → that expression is the *walk-away ceiling*, not the bid (at the ceiling the acquirer's value created is zero and the seller captures every synergy rupee; bid below it and state the margin you keep).
- **Error fixed:** "synergies = 60 a year ÷ 10% = 600, add 600" → discount for the two-year ramp: 600 / 1.1² ≈ 496 (counting run-rate synergies as if captured on day 1 inflates the walk-away price from ~1,400 to 1,500).
- Value means standalone intrinsic value. Take synergies at present value, after time-to-capture and probability.
- There are two lenses:
  - The **deal-lifecycle lens** (need → screening → diligence → valuation → execution → integration → exit) fits cases that centre on screening, diligence, integration or exit.
  - The **environment lens** (acquirer, target, market, synergies, risk) fits cases where M&A is one option inside a bigger question.
  - Combining them usually works best. Let the interviewer choose which branch to open. Details are in `case-types.md` under M&A.
- "Business overlap" belongs under synergies. "Individual and combined value" is valuation, not a type of synergy.
- Exit strategy is a branch only for financial buyers: a stake sale, a secondary sale to another fund, or a listing.
- Run valuation alongside diligence, not only at deal execution.
- Never recommend a deal without price guidance.

## Card 6 — Growth

**Keywords to capture & restate**
- Growth in revenue, profit or share? By how much, and by when?
- The existing business, new opportunities, or both? Are acquisitions allowed?

**Objective first**
"Revenue growth, profit growth or share, and against what baseline?" The job is to close the gap: target − organic baseline.

**Clarifier bank** (each answer changes the tree)
1. *Company.* What is the current business model and growth trend? This sets the baseline.
2. *Product.* Which products or services are in scope?
3. *Geography or segment.* Is growth expected in existing or new markets?
4. *Competition.* What is our current position? A leader can grow with the market; a laggard must take share.

Optional: budget, capacity, regulation.

**Equation spine**
`Growth = Existing business (P × V in current segments) + New opportunities (segments, geographies, products, channels)`, with M&A or JV as a *mode* for either. `Gap = Target − Baseline`.

**Approach buckets**
1. **Diagnose.** Revenue = P × V. Is the shortfall in price or in volume? Is the bottleneck demand or capacity?
2. **Existing business.**
   - Volume: more customers *in current segments*, purchase frequency, upsell and cross-sell, retention.
   - Price: premium positioning, value perception, cutting discount leakage.
   - Product and service improvements.
   - Distribution reach and availability.
3. **New opportunities.**
   - Geographies (Card 2).
   - New customer segments.
   - New products (Cards 3 and 4).
   - New channels.
4. **Inorganic.** M&A or JV (Card 5) can serve any branch above. Explain why you chose one over the other.
5. **Feasibility and priority.**
   - Incremental contribution vs investment, and payback.
   - Rank the levers by impact × feasibility, and check that they add up to the gap.

**Numbers that crack it** [ILLUSTRATIVE, ₹ crore]
- Revenue is 500 today and the target is 800 in three years, which needs a CAGR of (800/500)^(1/3) − 1 ≈ **17%**.
- The organic baseline grows 8% a year: 500 × 1.08³ ≈ **630**. The remaining **≈170** must come from new levers.
- A lever that needs 120 of investment and adds 40 a year of incremental contribution pays back in **3 years**.

**Customer-access check (4A)**
When volume is the gap, find the A that limits adoption and pull that lever first. Examples: availability in rural outlets, or an affordable small pack for price-constrained buyers.

**Risks to name**
- Demand overestimation.
- Capacity constraints.
- Competitive response.
- Execution capacity.
- Cannibalisation between levers.

**The close**
1. What should we do? The key levers, ranked and sized against the gap.
2. Why? Market plus capability.
3. How? The execution plan.
4. Risks and mitigations.

Time frame: quick wins, then scale and optimise, then expand and diversify.

**Coach tips**
- Size the gap first. The levers you recommend must add up to it.
- Under the existing business, "new customers" means more customers in current segments. New segments belong under new opportunities, so the tree stays MECE.
- M&A and JV are modes, not destinations. Do not put them beside "new product" and "new market" at the same level of the tree.
- Judge each lever on incremental contribution less incremental fixed cost, compared with its investment. A generic "Profit = Revenue − Cost" check does not do this.
- Growth cases reward flexibility and first-principles reasoning. Always finish with feasibility and risk.

## The 4A customer-access lens

The 4A lens asks whether customers who want the product can actually get it. It is the customer-side check on volume. Use it when demand should exist but sales do not follow.

| A | Definition | What to check | India examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Awareness** | Target customers know the offer exists and what it does. | Reach, aided and unaided recall, message clarity. | Rural media reach is thin, so haats and melas, wall painting, van campaigns and local influencers carry the message. |
| **Accessibility** | Customers can reach the offer, buy it and use it easily. | Distance to the point of sale, purchase steps, language, payment modes, usability. | Vernacular interfaces, UPI/QR payments at small outlets, assisted buying for first-time digital users, store hours that suit daily-wage customers. |
| **Affordability** | Customers can pay for it, given their income and cash-flow timing. | Price per use, pack size, financing, payment timing. | Small-SKU packs at ₹1–₹10 price points matched to daily cash flows; EMIs on two-wheelers and appliances; pay-per-use and prepaid plans. |
| **Availability** | The product is in stock where and when customers want to buy it. | Numeric distribution, stock-outs, fill rates, operating hours. | Direct distribution often stops at larger towns, and villages are served through sub-stockists and wholesalers. A product missing from village kirana shelves fails however strong the demand. |

**Keep the four distinct**
- **Accessibility** asks whether the customer can get to the offer and use it. **Availability** asks whether it is there when they arrive.
- **Affordability** is the customer's ability to pay. It is not whether our price beats a rival's, which belongs in the competition bucket.

**Where the lens sits in each case type**

| Case type | Where 4A goes | What it tests |
|---|---|---|
| Profitability | The internal volume driver (Card 1) | Why volume fell while the market held up. |
| Market entry | The "how to enter" bucket (Card 2) | Whether the go-to-market plan can reach the target segment. |
| New product launch | The launch plan (Card 3) | Awareness starts at zero; distribution must be in place before the advertising runs. |
| Pricing | Price-point and pack architecture (Card 4) | Whether the chosen price is within the customer's reach. |
| M&A | Revenue-synergy check (Card 5) | Whether the combination actually widens reach. |
| Growth | Volume levers (Card 6) | Which A limits adoption in current and new segments. |

**How to use it:** find the A that binds, fix that one first, and size the fix. A campaign that raises awareness is wasted if availability is the constraint.

## Recommendation guardrails

Run this six-point check before you speak the close. Each point catches a failure that interviewers mark down.

1. **Legal and ethical.** Never recommend agreeing prices, sharing out markets or exchanging price information with competitors. Setting your own price in response to a rival is legitimate; coordinating with that rival is collusion. Never propose getting around a regulation.
2. **Respects the constraints.** If the interviewer has ruled an option out (no price rise, no layoffs, no new capex), do not bring it back as the lead recommendation. If you believe the constraint is wrong, say so explicitly and separately.
3. **Realistic time horizons.** Place each action by how long it takes.
   - Near-term: hedging, renegotiating contracts, adding price-variation clauses, fixing stock-outs, tightening discounts.
   - Long-term: product redesign, material substitution that needs requalification, plant moves, brand repositioning.
   - Example: in a commodity input shock, hedging is the quick move and redesigning around a cheaper material is the slow one. Reversing the two is a common error.
4. **Matches the diagnosis.** Every recommendation must trace back to a finding. If the diagnosis is "we passed on less of the cost increase than peers did", the lead fix is pass-through, not an advertising campaign.
5. **Quantified.** Size each lever against the gap (₹, or pp of margin), and show that the levers add up to it.
6. **Names the binding risk.** State the single assumption that would flip the answer, and pair it with a mitigation or a test.

For the full close structure (answer first, 2–3 reasons, risks, next steps) and how it is scored, see `case-interview.md` (Step 7 and the Scoring Rubric). For the post-case debrief template, see `case-facilitation-and-scoring.md`.
